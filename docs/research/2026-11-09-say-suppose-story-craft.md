# Say Suppose: story-craft rules from great storytellers

2026-11-09. Research note for script writing and the five reviewer seats (viewer, story, fact, voice, craft).
Each rule is something a reviewer model can check on a script, with a one-line test. Sources were opened and read
for this note (WebFetch or WebSearch result pages); URLs are listed per rule. Wording is paraphrased; quotes are under
15 words. Where only a secondary page could be read (a memo summary, a review of a paper), the "Source" column says so.

Read alongside: `channels/say-suppose/style.md`, `docs/research/2026-11-06-say-suppose-reference-study.md`, the
pause-time video's G2 rounds 1-5 and G4 round 1, and the rules adopted 2026-11-08 (cut test; beats as runtime shares;
felt not taught; the five "but what about" questions; close on one real thing). "Existing rule" notes say where a source
**supports**, **sharpens** or **contradicts** what we already have. Rules that only restate an existing rule are left out.

The first video's failures these rules aim at: "it keeps stopping to teach me" (G4), "about 90 s with no event" after
the rescue (G2 r3), scroll-away at facts right after the payoff (G2 r2, r4; G4), the twist given away a scene early
(G2 r1 B7), montages that are moods, not events (G2 r3), and a gag that read as a copy (reference study, coffee mug).

### 2. Structure and escalation

| ID | Rule | Test (one line) | Seat | Source | URL | Existing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | **"Therefore" or "but" between every beat, never "and then".** Each beat is caused by the one before or complicates it. This includes the first-hours gags and every montage | Write the beat list with a connector between each pair. Any "and then" (or a connector that has to be forced) fails | story | Trey Parker and Matt Stone | https://www.huffpost.com/entry/matt-stone-trey-parker-stand-in_n_953696 ; https://speakola.com/arts/matt-stone-trey-parker-nyu-writing-class-2014 | Sharpens the cut test. Directly aimed at G2 r3's "moods, not things that happen" (the month-6 montage, the lift door). Contradicts the format table's "You use it for dumb things" if the dumb things are a list (fix: each gag causes or complicates the next) |
| S2 | **Every beat turns a value.** Note the charge at the start and end of each beat (safe/unsafe, together/alone, in control/not). A beat that ends where it began is cut or merged; exposition alone never justifies a beat | Beat sheet with value in and value out; any beat with in = out fails | story | Robert McKee, *Story* (scene turns a value; no turn, no scene) | https://sive.rs/book/Story ; https://emshort.blog/2019/02/05/story-robert-mckee/ | Supports and makes checkable the 2026-11-08 cut test |
| S3 | **Open a gap every time you use the power.** Before each use, the script states (or the figure shows) what you expect; the result differs at least in part. Tell the viewer the plan, then let the plan fail | For each use of the power write "you expect X / you get Y". Y = X in more than one use out of three fails | story | Robert McKee (gap between expectation and result); Matthew Dicks (backpacks: share the plan, then it fails) | https://sive.rs/book/Story ; https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2021/06/12/storyworthy-dicks.html | New. The pause-time first days were uses that simply worked (coffee, mug), which is why they read as a list |
| S4 | **A signpost of progress at least every 90 s**: new information, a new obstacle, a decision, or a change of direction on the main promise | List signposts with timestamps; any gap over 90 s fails | viewer, story | Brandon Sanderson (signposts of progress; lack of them is why readers quit) | https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2 | New, and checks G2 r3's "3:00 to 4:30 has no event" |
| S5 | **Re-engage near 3:00 and again near 6:00** with the biggest thing only this what-if can show (the rule device at full scale), in service of the story | Name the set piece in 2:30-3:30 and (for videos over 6 min) 5:30-6:30; none fails | viewer, craft | MrBeast memo (minute-3 and minute-6 re-engagement; "wow factor") | https://www.danielscrivner.com/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production-summary/ (summary of the leaked memo; the memo's authenticity is unconfirmed) | Supports the 3:30 turn; moves it earlier or adds a set piece before it. The pause-time video's 3:00-4:30 sag sits exactly where the memo says viewers leave |
| S6 | **Compress, don't walk.** In the first-hours beat, skip days: each use is a bigger consequence than the last, and no two uses sit at the same scale | Rank the first-hours uses by consequence; any repeat of scale, or a decrease, fails | story | MrBeast memo ("crazy progression"; "stop telling… start showing") | https://www.danielscrivner.com/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production-summary/ | Sharpens "three jumps, each bigger than the last" down to the gags inside a jump |
| S7 | **The power only solves what the viewer already understands.** Everything the climax uses was stated in the rule or shown working before the halfway mark. No new property of the power after 50%. Coincidence may get you into trouble, never out | List each property the climax depends on, with the line where it was first shown. Missing, or first shown after 50%, fails | story, fact | Brandon Sanderson, First Law; Emma Coats #19 | https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-first-law ; https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/ | Supports "State the change exactly, once" and makes it binding at the climax |
| S8 | **Limits are the story.** From the turn on, more lines go to what the power cannot do and what it costs than to what it can do | Count lines about abilities against lines about limits and costs after the turn; abilities more than limits fails | story | Brandon Sanderson, Second Law ("Limitations > Powers"; "Superman is his weaknesses") | https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-second-law | Supports "A person to lose" and G2 r2 S2 ("name the cost at 3:30") |
| S9 | **Deepen before you add.** After 60% of runtime, no new character, place or power rule appears unless it was planted earlier | List first appearances after 60%; any unplanted one fails | story | Brandon Sanderson, Third Law (expand what you have before adding); Emma Coats #5 (combine characters, skip detours) | https://alamrafiul.com/blogs/rules-of-magic-systems-sanderson/ (summary of his laws); https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/ | New. G2 r3 #2 and G4: the astronaut and road-designer facts were late additions |
| S10 | **Find the five-second moment and slow down into it.** Name the single moment the video exists for (in the first video, the fall on the stairs or "Somebody knows"). Put it as close to the end as the format allows. Just before it, lines get shorter, details more exact, gaps longer (the hourglass) | Name the moment; check the 20 s before it for shorter lines and more concrete detail than the 20 s before that | story, voice | Matthew Dicks (five-second moment; hourglass) | https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2021/06/12/storyworthy-dicks.html ; https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/59440320-storyworthy-engage-teach-persuade-and-change-your-life-through-the-p | Supports "Wordless peaks". Partly contradicts "the cost lands near 60%" (see conflict 6) |
| S11 | **Throw out the first five ideas for every gag.** The script's notes list rejected alternatives for each use of the power, and the obvious one is gone | For each gag, are at least three alternatives recorded? Is the chosen one something a reference channel has done? | story | Emma Coats #12 (and #9: list what would not happen next) | https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/ | New. The coffee-mug clash with Ifverse (reference study) is the failure this prevents |

### 3. Character and stakes

| ID | Rule | Test (one line) | Seat | Source | URL | Existing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | **You want something in every scene, and something stands in the way.** Before the power arrives you already want something small and concrete; every later scene has a want and an obstacle, and neither is leisurely | For each scene write "you want ___ / ___ stands in the way". A blank, or an obstacle that costs nothing, fails | story | Aaron Sorkin (intention and obstacle; "Somebody wants something…"); Kurt Vonnegut rule 3 (even a glass of water) | https://www.flyingwrestler.com/2023/12/intention-and-obstacle/ (Erik Bork quoting the MasterClass); https://lloydliterary.com/2009/08/23/eight-rules-for-writing-a-short-story/ | New for "you". The rule's choices once a minute are choices without wants unless this is checked |
| C2 | **Throw the opposite at you.** The cost lands on the thing you are most comfortable with or most sure of (the habit from H5), not on a random misfortune | Name your comfort or belief in act one and the beat that attacks it; unrelated cost fails | story | Emma Coats #6; Will Storr (the flawed belief the plot tests) | https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/ ; https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/science-of-storytelling-book-summary/ | Sharpens "A person to lose": the person is lost because of who you are, not by chance |
| C3 | **Trying beats winning.** At the climax the power alone is not enough; you add effort, a sacrifice or a choice, and it shows on the figure | At the climax, would the power alone have solved it? Yes fails | story, craft | Emma Coats #1 (we admire trying more than succeeding) | https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/ | New |
| C4 | **Pay a heavy price for what you found.** Each video has a "take" beat: after you get what you wanted, you pay for it, and the price is shown, not described | Point to the beat after the win where the price is paid on screen; none fails | story | Dan Harmon (story circle: find, then take, "pay a heavy price") | https://deckle.studio/wiki/workflow/organization/dan-harmons-story-circle/ | Supports the cost beat at about 60% |
| C5 | **Write to one person.** Each script names one viewer (age, city, phone at night) and every gag and example comes from that person's life | Each gag: would that viewer have done or seen this? Any "no" gets rewritten | viewer | Kurt Vonnegut rule 7 (write to please one person) | https://lloydliterary.com/2009/08/23/eight-rules-for-writing-a-short-story/ | Supports the reviewer persona already used; makes it a writing rule. G2 r1 B12 (a boss means nothing to a student) is the failure |
| C6 | **Keep "you" simple and the world specific.** The figure stays iconic (few face details) so the viewer can step into it; detail goes into the set, props and the other people | Does any line or scene note add detail to your face beyond the rig's brows, lids and mouths? | craft | Scott McCloud, *Understanding Comics* (a minimally detailed character eases identification) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding_Comics | Supports the rig as it is; argues against adding realism to "you" |

### 4. Curiosity and open loops

| ID | Rule | Test (one line) | Seat | Source | URL | Existing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | **Show the bomb under the table.** Before every peak except the final twist, the viewer learns the danger before you (or your person) do, at least 15 s ahead, and a clock is in the set | For each peak, find the line or shot where the viewer learns the danger and its time; under 15 s, or no visible clock or deadline, fails | story, craft | Alfred Hitchcock to Truffaut (fifteen seconds of surprise against fifteen minutes of suspense; inform the public, except for a twist) | https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/728496-there-is-a-distinct-difference-between-suspense-and-surprise-and ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themes_and_plot_devices_in_Hitchcock_films | Supports the split-flap counter at real speed. Sharpens it: the danger is planted before the counter starts, not when it does |
| Q2 | **Never zero open questions.** Every answer opens or reveals a new question; the next question is open before the current one closes | List open questions per 30 s. Any window with none fails | viewer, story | Ira Glass (bait: raise questions, answer along the way); George Loewenstein (sequences with an anticipated but unknown end) | https://www.visakanv.com/archives/2013/03/19/building-blocks-of-storytelling-ira-glass/ ; https://law.temple.edu/aer/2025/02/13/curiosity-compelled-the-cat-to-read-the-next-chapter/ | Sharpens the five "but what about" questions: raise each one (as your doubt or a stranger's look) before answering it |
| Q3 | **Give enough to guess.** Before posing a question, give one concrete anchor (a normal number, a familiar object) so the viewer can guess; a question asked into blank ignorance does not pull | For each question, quote the anchor line before it; none fails | viewer, fact | George Loewenstein (curiosity needs partial knowledge; gap theory) | https://psychologyfanatic.com/information-gap-theory/ ; https://scispace.com/papers/the-psychology-of-curiosity-a-review-and-reinterpretation-2bj8cmzgxx | Supports "a number against a normal number"; puts the normal number first |
| Q4 | **The turn breaks an expectation the script built.** The line or beat just before the turn makes the viewer (and you) expect something specific; the turn violates it | Quote the expectation line before the turn; if the turn breaks nothing stated, fail | story | Loewenstein (violation of expectations); Will Storr (unexpected change) | https://law.temple.edu/aer/2025/02/13/curiosity-compelled-the-cat-to-read-the-next-chapter/ ; https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/science-of-storytelling-book-summary/ | Sharpens "around 3:30 one thing you assumed turns out wrong": the assumption has to be said or shown first |
| Q5 | **Change the colour of the Elephant at the turn.** The question the video asks changes (from "what can I do with this?" to "what is it costing me?" or "who else has it?") and recasts what came before | Write the video's question at 0:30 and after the turn; same question fails | story | Matthew Dicks (changing the colour of the Elephant) | https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2021/06/12/storyworthy-dicks.html | New |
| Q6 | **Someone knows more than you.** At least once, the viewer sees that a character in the story knows something you don't | Point to the shot; none is a weak loop (optional for some topics) | story | Loewenstein (information possessed by someone else) | https://law.temple.edu/aer/2025/02/13/curiosity-compelled-the-cat-to-read-the-next-chapter/ | Supports the stranger, which reviewers named as the reason they stayed (G2 r2, r4) |
| Q7 | **Every plant fires; every payoff was planted.** A ledger of plants and payoffs: each prop or person given screen focus pays off, and the twist is planted at least three times without being named | Plant/payoff table; an orphan plant or an unplanted payoff fails; a twist with fewer than three plants fails | story, craft | Anton Chekhov (the loaded rifle); Lisa Cron (a pattern of hints makes a late reveal shocking and believable); Dicks (breadcrumbs that reveal too little to guess) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun ; https://sive.rs/book/WiredForStory ; https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2021/06/12/storyworthy-dicks.html | Sharpens "Plant and pay off" (one line) into a full ledger. "Three plants" is from G2 r4 (the stranger) and Cron's pattern of hints, not a number any source gives |
| Q8 | **Make a prediction that might be wrong.** Once per long section the narrator predicts something plausible ("By day twelve you'll have spent them all") and the story later keeps or breaks it | Find the predictions and where each resolves; an unresolved one fails | story, voice | Matthew Dicks (crystal ball) | https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2021/06/12/storyworthy-dicks.html | New |

### 5. Teaching without lecturing

| ID | Rule | Test (one line) | Seat | Source | URL | Existing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Misconception first.** Before each central fact, you act on the common wrong belief (you try the thing most viewers think would work); it fails; then the fact explains why. The confusion is resolved within two lines | For each central fact: is the wrong intuition acted or said first, and resolved within two lines? | fact, story | Derek Muller (PhD; refutation videos: more reported confusion, more learning; plain exposition can raise confidence in wrong ideas) | https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2018.00049/full (review citing Muller et al.); https://www.bobvanvliet.com/notes/designing-effective-multimedia-for-physics-education/ ; https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/11/05/the-element-of-truth.html | New, and the strongest fix for "it keeps stopping to teach me": the fact becomes the reason your plan failed. Pairs with S3 |
| T2 | **Exposition is ammunition.** A fact appears only at the beat it turns, least important first, the most important last. Never front-load facts "so you understand" | For each fact line, name the beat it turns. "Context" or "setup" fails; fact after the payoff it explains fails | fact, story | Robert McKee (exposition as ammunition, dramatize it) | https://sive.rs/book/Story | Sharpens "felt not taught". It is the rule G2 r1 B1 and G4 applied by hand (road stats after the rescue, 23 m moved before it) |
| T3 | **Action, then a short reflection, never two reflections in a row.** Alternate a sequence of actions with a moment saying what it means; the reflection is shorter than the action before it | Tag lines A (action) or R (reflection, fact, meaning). R next to R, or an R block longer than the A block before it, fails | story, voice | Ira Glass (anecdote and moment of reflection) | https://www.visakanv.com/archives/2013/03/19/building-blocks-of-storytelling-ira-glass/ | Supports "never two fact lines in a row" and extends it to non-fact reflections (the chronostasis run was reflections, not facts) |
| T4 | **One idea, one number per beat.** If a beat needs two numbers or two ideas, it is two beats or one gets cut | Count numbers and ideas per beat; over one fails | viewer, fact | MrBeast memo (for millions to understand it, it must be simple) | https://www.danielscrivner.com/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production-summary/ | Supports G2 r2 S1 and S4 (four figures in 25 s; three numbers back to back) |
| T5 | **Land the fact on a concrete detail.** Each fact arrives through one specific, drawable detail, not an abstraction | For each fact line, name the drawable detail; none fails | craft, voice | Chekhov's 1886 letter (seize on small details: the glint on broken glass); Lisa Cron (specifics, not generalities) | https://quoteinvestigator.com/2013/07/30/moon-glint/ ; https://sive.rs/book/WiredForStory | Supports "Facts are drawn" |
| T6 | **Two kinds of wow.** A visual wow in the first minute (something only this what-if can show) and the single most mind-boggling real fact saved for the long run, where the title's scale lands | Name both and their timestamps; a top fact spent in the first two minutes fails | viewer, fact | MrBeast memo (wow factor early); McKee (save the critical exposition for last); Andrew Stanton (the best stories infuse wonder) | https://www.danielscrivner.com/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production-summary/ ; https://sive.rs/book/Story ; https://singjupost.com/the-clues-to-a-great-story-by-andrew-stanton-at-ted-full-transcript/ | Sharpens "The long run… The honest answer lands here". Reconciles conflict 2 |
| T7 | **Start from something that confuses but delights.** The script's header names the one weird real fact or perspective the video grew from; it must appear in the story, not in a sidebar | Is the founding fact named, and is it lived by you in a scene? | fact | Michael Stevens, Vsauce (ideas start from weird facts or perspectives he wants to share) | https://amatranscripts.com/ama/michael_stevens_2014-04-21.html | New |

### 6. Endings

| ID | Rule | Test (one line) | Seat | Source | URL | Existing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | **Write the ending first.** The outline has the last image and last line before the middle is written; every beat's therefore/but chain reaches it | Does the outline's first version contain the final line? Can every beat be traced to it? | story | Emma Coats #7; Andrew Stanton (know your punchline) | https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/ ; https://singjupost.com/the-clues-to-a-great-story-by-andrew-stanton-at-ted-full-transcript/ | New (process) |
| N2 | **You end as the opposite of how you began, on one axis.** One line for you at 0:00 and one at the end; they are opposites (careless with time → guarding every second) | Write the two lines; not opposites fails | story | Dan Harmon (return, having changed); Matthew Dicks (the beginning is the opposite of the end); Lisa Cron (stories are about how we change) | https://deckle.studio/wiki/workflow/organization/dan-harmons-story-circle/ ; https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2021/06/12/storyworthy-dicks.html ; https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/2694174-the-reader-must-want-to-know-what-happens-next?tab=author | Sharpens "Back to you" |
| N3 | **Surprising but fulfilling.** The ending answers the title's promise in a way a cold reader cannot predict from the first 30% but, once seen, can trace to at least two plants | Give a fresh reviewer the first 30% and ask for the ending; if they get it, or if they cannot then find two plants, fail | story, viewer | Brandon Sanderson (payoff: a surprising but fulfilling answer); Hitchcock (a twist is the one place for surprise) | https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2 ; https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/728496-there-is-a-distinct-difference-between-suspense-and-surprise-and | Sharpens "End on a twist" |
| N4 | **Leave fast.** No wind-down after the final beat: no "so in the end", no summary, no early signal that it is ending. The real-world line sits inside the last image, then the end card | Search the last 30 s for summary phrases; time from the last story beat to the end card over about 10 s fails | viewer, voice | MrBeast memo (don't signal the ending; abrupt endings); William Goldman (late entry, and you can drop the ends of scenes) | https://www.danielscrivner.com/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production-summary/ ; https://kellymccullough.com/late-entry/ ; https://nevalalee.wordpress.com/tag/william-goldman/ | Supports G2 r1 B8 (narration after the blackout bleeds the chill). Tension with Glass and the "one real thing" close (conflict 3) |

### 7. Cinematic staging the script must specify

| ID | Rule | Test (one line) | Seat | Source | URL | Existing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V1 | **Enter every scene late.** The first line or shot of a scene is mid-action; no scene opens on you arriving, waking or walking in unless that is the event | First line of each `> SCENE:`: is it mid-action? | craft, story | William Goldman (late entry) | https://kellymccullough.com/late-entry/ ; https://thescriptlab.com/screenwriting/script-tips/620-scenes-start-late-get-out-early/ | New |
| V2 | **One idea per shot, unmistakably clear.** Each `> SCENE:` line names the one thing the viewer must look at; the set, light and camera point to it | Does each scene line name a single focus? Two foci fails | craft | Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas (staging, Disney's principles) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation | New |
| V3 | **Anticipate every big action.** Before a big move or a reveal, the script names the wind-up pose or the glance off-screen that prepares it | For each big action or entrance, is the anticipation named? | craft | Johnston and Thomas (anticipation) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation | New |
| V4 | **Face, what you see, face.** Key reveals are cut as your face, the thing, your face again; the second face is a named emotion. The music also colours the face | Each reveal in the script has the A-B-A pattern and a named reaction | craft | Lev Kuleshov and Hitchcock's "pure editing" demonstration; later studies (Mobbs 2006, Barratt 2016; music shifts face reading) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect | Supports "A face reaction follows every reveal within 2 seconds"; adds the return to the face and the cue for music |
| V5 | **Cut on the gap.** At peaks, show the cause and the effect and leave the moment between to the viewer (the step, then you at the bottom); never narrate the missing middle | At each peak, is a moment deliberately left out between two shots? Is it then described in words? (fail) | craft, story | Scott McCloud (closure: imagination joins two images into one idea); Andrew Stanton (give them 2 + 2, not 4) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding_Comics ; https://singjupost.com/the-clues-to-a-great-story-by-andrew-stanton-at-ted-full-transcript/ | Supports "Wordless peaks" |
| V6 | **Emotion beats continuity.** When a cut or staging choice must choose, keep the felt moment and sacrifice spatial continuity first (a jump in geography or a cheat in scale is fine) | Has any felt beat been trimmed or flattened for neatness or continuity? ## Conflicts

**Between sources**

1. **Give it all away, or hold it back?** Vonnegut's rule 8 says give readers as much as possible as early as possible
   ("To hell with suspense"). Stanton calls good story "a well-organised absence of information" and Loewenstein
   says curiosity is a felt gap. Hitchcock is on Vonnegut's side about *danger* (tell the audience about the bomb) and
   on Stanton's about the twist. Our resolution: tell the viewer the situation, the rule and the danger early (Q1, S7);
   hold back the *answer* and the *conclusion* (Q2, V5, W2).
2. **Spend the wow early, or save the best for last?** The MrBeast memo front-loads spectacle (a house lifted by crane
   in the first 30 s). McKee saves the most important exposition for last, and Dicks puts the key moment as close to
   the end as possible. Resolution in T6: the *visual* wow comes early; the most important *truth* comes late.
3. **End abruptly, or reflect?** The memo wants abrupt endings and Goldman cuts the ends of scenes; Glass says a story
   needs its moment of reflection, and the channel closes on "one real thing" plus a comment question. Resolution in
   N4: the reflection is one line inside the last image, never a summary after it.
4. **Conflict as the engine, or a turn without conflict?** Parker and Stone, Sorkin and McKee drive everything through
   conflict. Kishōtenketsu (introduction, development, twist, reconciliation) needs no conflict and turns on a change
   of perspective (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu). For a what-if, the first-hours play can run as
   ki and shō, and the 3:30 turn as ten. But G2 r3 showed play without obstacle sags, so S1 and C1 still apply from the
   first use of the power.
5. **"Every day…" or start near the end?** Coats's spine and Storr's theory of control want the normal day
   shown; Vonnegut (start close to the end), Goldman (late entry), the memo and the owner (no empty opening) want
   action first. Resolution in H5: the routine is one line or one picture inside the cold open, never a scene.
6. **Where the five-second moment sits.** Dicks wants it near the end; our format puts the cost at about 60% and the
   choice at 75-80%. Make the *choice* (or the twist) the five-second moment and give it the hourglass (S10). The cost
   at 60% stays as the "take" (C4).
7. **Confusion helps, or simple wins?** Muller's refutation videos left students more confused but they learned more;
   the memo says the simpler the better, and our viewers left at the teaching. They fit if the confusion is a story
   event (your plan failed) and resolves within two lines (T1). A confusing explanation is never the fix.
8. **How strong is the Kuleshov effect?** Prince and Hensley (1992) failed to replicate it; Mobbs (2006) and Barratt
   (2016) found it. V4 therefore asks for an acted reaction, not a neutral face that relies on the cut.
9. **Chekhov's gun against red herrings.** The channel wants rewatch plants; a red herring breaks Chekhov's rule unless
   it pays off as misdirection. Q7 allows a misdirection only if the script pays it off.

**With the channel's existing rules**

1. **"Something changes every 2 to 4 seconds; no hold longer than 6 seconds"** (style.md, Look) against Dicks's
   hourglass, Murch's emotion first, Galloway's "let it breathe", and our own "8 to 10 wordless seconds" at the
   biggest beat. The owner cut the stairs-fall hold from 8 s to 4.5 s at G4, so in practice the 6 s cap won. The note
   recommends: keep motion changing every 2-4 s even inside a wordless peak (slow push, colour drain, a hand), so the
   peak can be long without a static hold. The owner should decide which rule governs the peak.
2. **"You use it for dumb things"** (format table, 0:50-2:30) invites an "and then" list. Under S1, S3 and S6 each use
   must cause, complicate or out-scale the last.
3. **"Humour breaks tension, about once a minute in the first half"** risks breaking Sanderson's tone promise if humour
   then vanishes. H4 asks for one dry beat after the turn as well.
4. **"End on a twist and one question"** plus the 2026-11-08 "close on one real thing" plus the comment invitation is
   three endings. N4 and Hitchcock's twist rule suggest the order: twist, the one real thing (one line, inside the last
   image), then the comment question on the end card, as G2 r5 already did.
5. **"The long run: the scale the title deserves: the planet"** pulls away from you. Cron says stories are about how
   the protagonist changes, not the world. The "felt not taught" rule already says to land each fact on you; N2 makes
   the ending about you as well.
6. **"State the change exactly, once"** is backed by Sanderson's First Law (S7), but Q4 needs the rule to *build an
   expectation* the turn can break. Say the rule once; let your first uses teach the viewer what to expect.
7. **"About once a minute the narrator puts a choice to you"** is backed by Coats #13 and Sorkin, but a choice with no
   want behind it is filler. C1 makes the want explicit.
