# Part A

## 1. Draft production and cadence plan

### What is weak or wrong

> “About 12 hours of agent wall time and 70 minutes of owner time a video.”

Those are targets, not demonstrated measurements. At seven releases a week, that is 84 agent-hours, more than eight owner-hours, seven final renders, publishing work, slate review and inevitable reopened gates. Three concurrent sessions hide elapsed time; they do not remove render capacity, coordination or owner context-switching.

A three-video buffer is also too small. One difficult script, failed render, sick day or broken Chrome automation consumes the entire margin.

Other weaknesses:

- Seven owner gates are incompatible with a reliable daily operation.
- Five topics packaged at the weekend cannot react quickly to changing demand or lessons from newly published videos.
- “One 1080p render” leaves no correction budget when the final render exposes a seam, timing error or encoding problem.
- Merging kit changes daily while three videos use separate worktrees creates version drift, invalid caches and regressions.
- “Dubs never set the release date” produces an inconsistent Hinglish offering unless there is a firm later-delivery SLA.
- The estimates ignore thumbnail revisions, title changes, claims disputes, source replacement and upload failures.
- A major beat-caching/audio refactor before video two’s rough cut is itself a cadence threat.

### What is missing

1. **A defined initial viewer.** “A 15-year-old and a 40-year-old would both click” is too broad. It encourages generic writing.
2. **A channel distinction.** Why subscribe to Say Suppose instead of Ifverse, IFOLOGY or the next clone channel? “Sourced facts and paper-cut animation” is production description, not yet a viewer promise.
3. **An analytics loop.** Every release needs a 24-hour and seven-day review covering impressions, CTR by surface, first-30-second retention, major dips, average percentage viewed, returning viewers and comments—not merely music cues.
4. **A quality floor.** The plan says when to publish, not when to cancel or postpone.
5. **A fast lane for perishable topics.** A news-pegged AI idea cannot pass through the same long conveyor as an evergreen story.
6. **A launch experiment plan.** The first ten videos should test different promises, emotional tones and thumbnail constructions.
7. **Capacity for rework.** A production plan running at nominal maximum capacity will fail.

### What is risky

- Model “rooms” may reduce obvious mistakes, but they cannot predict real retention. They can also homogenize every script into the same rubric-compliant voice.
- A frame every two seconds cannot assess animation timing, transitions, comic rhythm or sound-picture synchronization.
- Publishing daily before the format is learned can generate ten weak signals instead of three strong ones.
- Daily releases may compete for the same tiny initial audience and reduce the learning window.
- The manual Studio steps remain a single fragile bottleneck.
- Making a Short about Delhi air may attract a different audience that does not convert to long-form story viewers.

### Concrete changes

- Start at **three videos a week or every other day**.
- Permit daily only after:
  - two consecutive weeks sustain the throughput,
  - the owner averages no more than one substantial reopen per video,
  - and **7–10 completed videos** are scheduled.
- Reduce owner gates to four:
  1. package and premise,
  2. script and claims,
  3. rough cut,
  4. final package and publish.
- Treat model reviews as QA before those gates, not additional approval authorities.
- Pin each video to a kit version. Promote kit changes on a controlled schedule, not continuously.
- Keep one reusable visual or audio innovation per video; do not rebuild the engine on the critical path.
- Allow two final-render attempts in the schedule.
- Give the dub a clear SLA—day-one, within 24 hours, or temporarily suspended.
- Review the slate after every two releases during the first ten, not only weekly.

### What matters most

- Preview ladder and audio-only revisions.
- Reusable sets, actions and props.
- Packaging before production.
- A real buffer and fallback cadence.
- Instrumenting actual production time.
- Separating picture and audio.

### What is mostly noise

- Exact model-family seat assignments.
- Elaborate “blind room” ceremony.
- Cloud-render optimization before the format proves demand.
- Precise owner-time estimates before a second complete video exists.

## 2. Story rules

### What is weak or wrong

There are far too many mandatory tests. A script cannot satisfy dozens of binary rules without becoming predictable “retention soup.”

The largest problem is confusing useful principles with universal laws:

- “A person to lose” forces melodrama into premises that may work better through wonder, embarrassment or identity.
- “A ladder of three jumps” will make every episode feel structurally identical.
- “A wrong belief it breaks” can force false contrarianism.
- “End on a twist” makes twists predictable.
- “A change every 2–4 seconds” encourages visual twitchiness and greatly increases animation work.
- “15–20% wordless” is an arbitrary quota.
- “Every beat turns a value” is too strict for short connective beats.
- “A visible clock” before every peak is absurdly repetitive.
- “Never zero open questions” risks manipulative, exhausting narration.
- “Three plants” is not inherently better than one excellent plant.
- Exact set pieces near minute three and minute six conflict with “no length cap.”
- Throwing out several gag ideas for every use is incompatible with daily cadence.

The craft note also turns weak secondary sources—summaries, Wikipedia, student blogs and an unverified memo—into hard production law. One cited transcript is labelled 2027 in a note dated November 2026; that is a source-hygiene warning.

### What is missing

- Permission for episodes to use different shapes: comic escalation, mystery, tragedy, wonder or social thought experiment.
- A test for Arthur’s distinctive voice. The rules optimize structure more than personality.
- A “delight” criterion: is any scene enjoyable independent of the lesson?
- Cold-reader testing of title expectation versus actual story.
- A rule against manufactured emotional stakes.
- A practical maximum length for the launch period.
- Post-publication validation of which supposed craft rules correlate with retention.

### What is risky

- Every story starts ordinary, escalates through three scales, threatens a loved one, turns at 60%, forces a choice at 80%, then twists. Viewers will learn the template rapidly.
- Dense open-loop engineering can make Arthur sound like an algorithm rather than a storyteller.
- Requiring every fact to arise from a failed action may produce contrived behaviour.
- A “person to lose” can feel emotionally exploitative when inserted only to satisfy the card.
- Animation requirements implied by the rules will stop daily cadence before writing does.

### Concrete change: reduce the mandatory rules to eight

1. **Confirm the click immediately.**
2. **Give “you” a concrete want.**
3. **Connect major beats with therefore/but.**
4. **Escalate consequences rather than merely changing locations.**
5. **Make facts change an action, decision or relationship.**
6. **Build toward one memorable emotional or visual moment.**
7. **Pay off the premise honestly; do not invent stakes the premise does not earn.**
8. **End quickly after the final change in perspective.**

Keep everything else as an optional technique library.

For the first ten videos, use a soft target of roughly **5–7 minutes**. Longer is allowed only when the beat sheet demonstrates why. “No length cap” is directionally sensible but operationally dangerous for a new channel.

### Highest-value rules

- H1: confirm the click.
- S1: therefore/but causality.
- C1: want and obstacle.
- S3/S6: expectation gaps and escalation.
- T1/T2: misconception or fact dramatized in action.
- S10: one defining moment.
- W2: do not narrate what the picture already says.
- N4: leave fast.

### Low-value or harmful rules

- Exact 30-second, 90-second, three-minute and six-minute checkpoints.
- A clock before every peak.
- A prediction in every long section.
- Three plants for every twist.
- Fixed humour, wordless and visual-change quotas.
- Binary failure because a small beat lacks a value reversal.
- Mandatory “person to lose.”

## 3. Music and sound rules

### What is weak or wrong

This is another oversized rulebook for a daily channel. A full custom score, motif architecture, detailed cue sheet, generated cues, recorded effects, rights ledger and automated spectral analysis per episode will consume substantial production time.

Several rules overreach their evidence:

- A comment about multi-minute silence becomes a ban on two seconds without score.
- WCAG’s 20 dB figure is being treated as a universal cinematic mix prescription.
- “Every visible event gets a sound” conflicts directly with avoiding mickey-mousing and density.
- Playing the channel motif at every rule-device use will make it irritating quickly.
- “At most two silences,” “one hit per act” and “music state changes every 60 seconds” are arbitrary.
- Not every pitched effect needs to match the musical key.
- Writing the final spotting sheet at G2 is premature. Exact spotting belongs after the rough cut.

The cue-sheet schema is overdesigned. It risks more time maintaining metadata than improving the emotional result.

### What is missing

- A separate mix check for the Hinglish performance, whose timing and voice spectrum will differ from Arthur’s.
- Testing on a cheap Android phone, earbuds and a noisy room—not just one phone at 30%.
- A reusable licensed music library strategy.
- A policy for when an episode deserves custom music.
- Dialogue consistency across videos.
- A contingency if generated music receives a Content ID claim.

### What is risky

The unresolved Higgsfield/ElevenLabs and reference-audio rights questions are a **stop-ship issue**, not a future administrative task. “Probably fine” is not sufficient for a public commercial channel.

Continuous underscore is also a retention risk. It can make every beat feel equally important and exhaust phone listeners.

### Concrete changes

- Build a small reusable palette:
  - 3–5 beds,
  - one tension pulse,
  - one loss cue,
  - one wonder cue,
  - a few branded effects.
- Allow at most **one custom hero cue** per ordinary episode.
- Record musical intent at G2, but do final spotting at G4.
- Reduce `cues.yaml` to boundary, intent, source, level, rights and key SFX.
- Use relative level rules as starting points, then judge speech intelligibility by ear.
- Do not force music under every moment.
- Use the motif at the ident only if the ident itself does not delay the hook; then one meaningful return is enough.
- Clear the existing theme’s rights and model-input rights before publication, or replace it.

### What matters most

- Clear narration.
- Emotional contrast.
- Music changes that do not spoil reveals.
- A few recognizable brand sounds.
- Phone-speaker testing.
- Rights documentation.
- No clicks or abrupt tails.

### What is noise

- Exact motif counts.
- Exact silence counts.
- In-key stinger lint.
- Spectrum-dip automation before basic mix judgement is reliable.
- One large hit per act.
- Original score per video as a universal rule.

## 4. Topic scoring mechanism

### What is weak or wrong

The 100-point score gives false precision to noisy evidence.

Major problems:

- Old five-million-view videos are weak evidence of present demand.
- Current channel medians are poor denominators for old comparables.
- Ratios from tiny channels are unstable.
- Demand, momentum and novelty repeatedly count the same breakout event.
- India gets four points through a crude proxy despite the English channel also targeting global viewers.
- Production receives 20 points—almost as much as demand—so cheap ideas can outrank better videos.
- Risk is treated as lost audience value when it should often be a review flag.
- The novelty gate is too strict. New channels usually grow through familiar demand with a distinctive promise, not untouched premises.
- “Two minutes a topic” is not credible for search, medians, autocomplete, news, source quality and fit review.
- Narrative potential and thumbnail strength are barely represented.

The largest omission is this: **the scorer evaluates topics more than videos**. “Immortality” is not the same product as “you remain 25 while your mother grows old.”

### What is missing

- Strength of the emotional engine.
- Strength of the first 30 seconds.
- Distinctiveness of the actual title/thumbnail pair.
- Evidence confidence.
- Fit with the intended core viewer.
- Whether the premise produces five strong scenes rather than three facts.
- Performance feedback from Say Suppose itself.
- A kill threshold for weak packaging.

### Concrete replacement

Use five scored dimensions, each with low/medium/high evidence confidence:

| Dimension | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Click promise and mobile thumbnail | 25 |
| Story engine and retention potential | 25 |
| Current demand evidence | 20 |
| Distinctive angle | 20 |
| Feasibility within cadence | 10 |

Use momentum as a ±5 modifier. Treat health, politics, tragedy and rights as review flags, not automatic deductions.

Gate only for:

1. truthful package,
2. sourceable central claims,
3. executable production,
4. clear viewer payoff,
5. no close copying of another video’s package or story.

Do not reject a premise merely because a neighbour covered it. Reject an undifferentiated version.

For each finalist, require three genuinely different title/thumbnail concepts. The best package should be chosen before the score is finalized.

### What matters most

- Outliers repeated across multiple channels.
- Recent velocity.
- A familiar premise with an unexpected personal rule.
- A package readable on a phone.
- Strong story consequences.
- Feasibility.

### What is mostly noise

- Exact 100-point totals.
- Tie-breaking by India autocomplete.
- Hard no-back-to-back rules.
- Eight-year demand ceilings.
- Treating a low-risk, cheap topic as inherently better.

# Part B

## First batch

1. **What If You Stopped Aging While Everyone Else Did?**  
   Best combination of cheap production, instant comprehension and genuine emotion. The draft title is too long; **“STILL 25”** is a strong tag.

2. **What If AI Took Your Job—and Everyone’s?**  
   Publish while the news peg is alive, but make the story about purchasing power and identity, not forecast statistics. The title is strong; **“DONE BY 9AM”** promises productivity, not displacement—use **“NO JOBS LEFT.”**

3. **What If You Understood Every Animal?**  
   Broad, warm and unusually rich in real mechanisms. The draft title is generic but clear; **“HE SAID WHAT?”** works if the dog’s expression carries the thumbnail.

4. **What If Your Clone Was Born Today?**  
   The baby twist is the idea and should be in the package. **“MY CLONE?”** is serviceable; **“THAT’S YOU”** is stronger.

5. **What If You Woke Up and the Moon Was Gone?**  
   Strong global image, Indian cultural recognition and a clean shift from personal to planetary scale. The revised title is more second-person; **“WHERE’D IT GO?”** is good.

## Second batch

6. **What If You Could Breathe Underwater?**  
   Proven demand and excellent visuals, but do it only after the sea kit is production-ready. The title is commodity packaging; **“NO AIR NEEDED”** merely explains it. Try **“DON’T COME UP.”**

7. **What If You Won the Lottery Tomorrow?**  
   Highly relatable and supported by a useful misconception reversal. The title is strong; **“ALL SIX”** is culturally narrow—**“DON’T TELL THEM”** creates a relationship stake.

8. **What If You Remembered Everything?**  
   Lower measured demand, but one of the strongest emotional stories and cheap to build. **“NEVER FORGET”** is clichéd; try **“YOU SAID THIS.”**

9. **What If You Were Invisible for a Day?**  
   Strong demand but badly flooded. The title and **“CAN’T SEE ME”** are generic; the thumbnail needs the blindness or missing-parent consequence, not a standard invisible-body gag.

10. **What If You Woke Up to a World With No Money?**  
    UPI gives it a distinctive Indian rung while the broader mechanism remains global. **“₹0 EVERYWHERE”** is strong for India but narrows the global package; **“ZERO EVERYWHERE”** travels better.

A promising addition for the next research pool is **“What If You Could See Every Lie?”** It has a clear visual rule, everyday use and relationship consequences, but it needs demand and neighbour checks before ranking.

## Where I disagree with the scores and proposed order

- **Talk to animals at 87 is inflated.** AI animal-translation coverage is adjacent to the fantasy, not the same promise. Production is also understated: multiple readable animal puppets and reactions are not an 18/20 easy build. It remains a top-three idea.
- **AI jobs at 83 double-counts one hype cycle.** Its emotional and age appeal are narrower than the score suggests, and forecast uncertainty is substantial.
- **UBI at 79 is much too high.** It is abstract, policy-heavy and difficult to dramatize through “you.” Old Kurzgesagt demand does not prove this package. I would hold it outside the first ten unless the rewrite becomes a personal story rather than an illustrated debate.
- **Aging’s score understates the strength of “no one else did,”** but the flood makes the plain title dangerous.
- **Clone production is not 20/20.** A baby, multiple ages and two versions of “you” still require animation work, though the story angle is excellent.
- **Moon production is underrated in difficulty.** Tides, coast, night lighting and planetary scale are not a simple build.
- **Remember everything is undervalued** because the rubric barely rewards emotional story potential.
- **Invisible should rank below remembering everything** because it is flooded and its scientific reversal is already familiar.

I agree with the proposed first three only as a practical sequence: aging is fast, AI is perishable, and the animal rig can be built alongside them. I would replace **UBI at #4** with **clone**, move **Moon to #5**, and keep UBI on hold. Sunk script work is not a reason to spend one of a new channel’s first releases.

# Part C

## Top 10 changes, ranked by impact

1. **Drop daily as the launch requirement.** Prove three-per-week or alternate-day quality first; require a 7–10-video buffer before daily.
2. **Define one core viewer and one clear channel promise.** “Everyone from 15 to 40” is not targeting.
3. **Cut the craft rulebook to eight mandatory principles.** Keep the rest as optional techniques.
4. **Reduce seven owner gates to four.** Model reviews are QA, not additional governance.
5. **Replace the 100-point topic score with a package-and-story rubric using confidence levels.**
6. **Hold UBI and lead with personal, visual premises.** Early releases should demonstrate the channel’s entertainment promise.
7. **Set a 5–7-minute launch target.** Earn exceptions; do not begin with unrestricted length.
8. **Create a full analytics loop after every release.** Let actual viewers retire or strengthen rules.
9. **Use reusable music and visual kits, with one custom centerpiece per video.** Daily bespoke production is unsustainable.
10. **Resolve music rights and dub delivery before scaling.** Unclear commercial rights are a release blocker, not paperwork for later.
