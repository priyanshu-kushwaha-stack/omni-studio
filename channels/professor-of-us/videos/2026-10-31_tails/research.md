# Research brief: What happened to our tails? (Short, ~50 s)

Researcher run 2026-10-31. Every claim id points to a row in `claims.md`. All 24 quotes there were checked by script against the saved source text.

## Thesis
You grew a tail and lost it before you were two months old, and a 2024 study suggests a single "jumping gene" in the ancestor of all apes helped switch it off, possibly at a price we still pay.

## Verdict

**Solid (say plainly)**
- Human embryos have a tail around weeks 4-6, longest at the end of week 5 (about 1/6 of the embryo), gone by about week 8 (c01-c03).
- The tail disappears partly because its cells die and are cleared by macrophages (c04).
- Humans and all apes lack an external tail; the loss is inferred at about 25 million years ago (c06, c07).
- Xia et al. 2024 (Nature): an Alu insertion in a TBXT intron causes hominoid-specific exon skipping; mice making both versions had short or no tails (c08-c11).
- Coccyx: 3-5 vertebrae; pelvic-floor muscles and gluteus maximus attach; one leg of the sitting tripod (c15-c17).
- True human tails are boneless and very rare, fewer than 40 reported (c18, c19).

**Hedged (say with "may", "suspect", "probably")**
- The Alu "may have contributed"; other changes probably locked it in, so undoing it wouldn't bring a tail back (c08, c12).
- The neural-tube "price" is the authors' speculation from mice (c13, c14).
- Why tails were lost: unknown; the paper says "probably" locomotion (c22).
- Fossil date: tailless by at least about 18 million years ago, from a secondary citation (c23).

**Must-not-say**
- "Apoptosis" for the embryo tail: the one cell-level study opened saw necrotic-looking death in the tail (c05).
- "Scientists found THE gene that deleted our tail" / "one mutation erased it".
- "Losing our tail causes spina bifida" or "this is why spina bifida exists".
- "We lost our tails so we could walk upright" (the first tailless apes were still four-legged branch walkers, c23).
- "The coccyx is 3-5 *fused* vertebrae" as a rule (fusion varies, c15); "you sit on it when leaning back" (unsourced, dropped).
- "Proconsul, 20 million years ago" (use "about 18", c23).
- "A baby's tail is a throwback to monkeys" (c19 says it's leftover embryo tail).

## Facts (each ends with a claim id from claims.md)
- About weeks 4-6 after conception, the embryo has a tail with 10-12 caudal segments (c01).
- At the end of week 5 the tail is about one-sixth of the embryo's length (c02).
- By the end of week 8 it is fully gone (c03).
- Cells throughout the tail die from stage 17 on, in "massive numbers" by stages 18-19; macrophages carry the debris into the body; the trunk also overgrows its base (c04).
- That cell death looks necrotic, not apoptotic, in one ultrastructural study of 4-6-week embryos (c05).
- Hominoids (humans + apes, including gibbons) lost the external tail; most monkeys kept it (c06).
- The loss is inferred at about 25 million years ago, when apes split from Old World monkeys (c07).
- An Alu element, a "jumping gene", landed in an intron of TBXT in the hominoid ancestor and pairs with an older, reversed Alu nearby (c08).
- That pairing makes cells skip exon 6, so apes make two TBXT versions (c09).
- Mice engineered to make both versions had shortened tails or none, depending on the ratio (c10).
- In one cross, all 19 mice had no external tail (c11).
- The insertion was probably not the only change; reversing it today would unlikely regrow a tail (c12).
- Some mice making the short version had neural-tube-closure defects resembling spina bifida (c13).
- The authors speculate tail loss carried an "adaptive cost": neural tube defects, about 1 in 1,000 newborns today (c14).
- The coccyx is 3-5 vertebral bodies, fused or not (c15).
- Levator ani, coccygeus and other pelvic-floor muscles attach in front; gluteus maximus behind (c16).
- Sitting, it is one leg of a tripod with the two ischial tuberosities (c17).
- True human tails: boneless, fat, muscle, vessels, nerves; fewer than 40 cases reported (c18).
- Doctors view them as a persisting bit of the embryo's tail, not a reversion (c19).
- About half of tail-like appendages come with a hidden spinal defect (c20).
- Longest reported: 18 cm, on a 17-year-old who had hidden it (c21).
- Why apes lost tails is "not clear"; probably locomotion as lifestyles changed (c22).
- Proconsulids were tailless by at least ~17.9 Mya while still walking on top of branches on all fours (c23).
- Tail loss/reduction happened independently in lorises, mandrills and some macaques (c24).

## Top 3 surprises (these drive the hook, the re-engagements and the payoff)
1. **You had a tail.** A sixth of your body at week five, gone by week eight, its cells killed off and eaten by your own immune cells (c01-c04). Hook line: "You've already lost a tail once."
2. **A jumping gene probably helped do it.** One stray stretch of DNA landing inside a tail gene ~25 Mya, and mice given the same setup lost theirs (c07-c11).
3. **It may have come with a price.** The same change gave some mice spina-bifida-like defects; the researchers suspect we still pay a little of that bill (c13, c14). Bonus counter-intuitive beat: the first tailless apes weren't walking upright at all; they were still on all fours on branches (c23).

## Three angles considered
1. **"You already lost a tail once" (embryo first, then evolution).** Personal, visual, loops naturally back to the tailbone you're sitting on. Recommended.
2. **"The jumping gene that took our tail" (2024 paper first).** Strong, but Be Smart, Vin Explains and Thomas Mulligan Shorts (all 2026) likely already lead with it.
3. **"Your tailbone isn't useless" (myth-buster).** Solid but small; works as the closing beat, not the whole Short.

## Best twist for a loop
End on the coccyx/sitting tripod: "...and right now, you're sitting on what's left of it." Open on "You've already lost a tail once." The last line ("sitting on what's left of it") lands the viewer back on the first frame (embryo with tail), so the loop reads as cause and effect. Alternative ending: "Losing it may have cost us something. We're still paying." (hedged, c14) into the opening question.
