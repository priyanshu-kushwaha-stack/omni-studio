# Research: nobody could say no to you

Fetched and read on 2026-11-11 (pre-G2). Every spoken claim, its exact quote and its source are in `claims.md`; each
quote was checked by script, character for character, against the fetched page text (PDF line breaks joined with a
single space). This file is the reading behind them, what we may and must not say, and what was looked at and not used.

## What the sources say

- **People say yes more than you think (c01, c02).** Flynn and Lake, "If you need help, just ask", Journal of
  Personality and Social Psychology 95(1), 128-143, 2008. Francis J. Flynn (then Columbia) and Vanessa K. B. Lake
  (then Columbia; she now publishes as Vanessa K. Bohns, Cornell, so "Flynn and Bohns" is the same paper). Six
  studies. Study 1: 42 students had to get 5 strangers to fill out a questionnaire; they predicted approaching 20.5
  people, it took 10.5, "approximately twice". Study 2: borrowing a stranger's cell phone (predicted 10.1, needed 6.2)
  and asking to be walked to the gym (7.2 vs 2.3). Study 3: charity fundraisers (210.3 vs 122.2 people). Abstract:
  across the first 3 studies people underestimated compliance "by as much as 50%". The cause (Study 6): askers weigh
  the cost of saying yes, not the social cost of saying no.
  - What we may say: strangers agree to small direct requests more often than we expect; askers expected to ask
    about twice as many people; underestimated by up to half.
  - Must not say: "most people say yes to anything"; "50%" as an average; anything about big or harmful requests.
- **The empty "because" (c03, c04).** Langer, Blank and Chanowitz, JPSP 36(6), 635-642, 1978. Library copier at
  CUNY's Graduate Center; the experimenter asked to cut in. Table 1, small favour (5 pages): no reason 60% (n 15),
  "because I have to make copies" 93% (n 15), "because I'm in a rush" 94% (n 16). Big favour (20 pages): no reason
  24% (n 25), empty reason 24% (n 25), real reason 42% (n 24). The authors: once the favour took effort, "the reason
  now seemed to matter".
  - What we may say: for a small ask, an empty reason worked about as well as a real one; for a bigger ask it didn't.
  - Must not say: "because" works on anything; any wording that hides the small groups (15-25 people) if a number
    is given on screen with n.
- **Paid 1 dollar vs 20 (c05).** Festinger and Carlsmith, "Cognitive consequences of forced compliance", Journal of
  Abnormal and Social Psychology 58, 203-210, 1959 (York University's Classics in the History of Psychology). An hour
  of dull tasks (spools, then pegs); then paid $1 or $20 to tell a waiting student it was fun. Enjoyment on a -5 to
  +5 scale: Control -.45, One Dollar +1.35, Twenty Dollar -.05. 20 per condition.
  - What we may say: those paid less later rated the dull task more enjoyable.
  - Must not say: "they loved it" (+1.35 out of 5 is mildly positive); "they believed the lie completely".
- **Milgram (c06, c07).** Milgram, "Behavioral study of obedience", Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 67(4),
  371-378, 1963 (scan, MIT course copy). 40 men, 20 to 50 (Simply Psychology). 26 of 40 went to the last switch (65%);
  the 14 who stopped all went at least to 300 volts. Panel: 30 switches, 15 to 450 volts, labels Slight Shock,
  Moderate Shock, Strong Shock, Very Strong Shock, Intense Shock, Extreme Intensity Shock, Danger: Severe Shock, then
  two marked XXX. The manuscript was received on December 27, 1961. Simply Psychology (updated 2026-11-08) says the
  18 variations (636 people) ran at Yale "between 1961 and 1962", and summarises the critiques (Perry 2013, Gibson
  2013: the experimenter improvised prods).
  - What we may say: published 1963; 26 of 40, 65%; went to the last switch, 450 volts; the shocks were fake.
  - Must not say: "65% of people would kill on orders"; a run month; that the result is uncontested.
- **Asch (c08, c09).** No primary fetched (Asch 1951 is a book chapter). Simply Psychology (updated 2026-09-21) and
  Wikipedia agree: about three quarters conformed at least once (75% / 74%), about a quarter never did, about a third
  of critical answers went with the wrong majority (32% / 35.7% / "about one-third" depending on paper). Alone, almost
  no errors (under 1%). Wikipedia notes textbooks overplay conformity: "95% of subjects defied the majority at least
  once".
  - What we may say: about three in four went along at least once; about a third of the answers.
  - Must not say: "three quarters of people conform"; a decimal figure.
- **Autonomy (c10).** Center for Self-Determination Theory, theory page (Deci and Ryan): basic psychological needs
  "for autonomy, competence, and relatedness"; thwarted needs go with ill-being.
  - What we may say: in self-determination theory autonomy is a basic need, alongside competence and relatedness.
  - Must not say: a number, or "proven law".
- **Overjustification (c11).** Lepper, Greene and Nisbett, JPSP 28(1), 129-137, 1973 (scan). Bing Nursery School,
  Stanford; children who already liked drawing with magic markers. Promised a "Good Player Award" (a certificate with
  a gold seal and ribbon), they later spent 8.59% of free time drawing, against 16.73% (no award) and 18.09%
  (surprise award). 51 children.
  - What we may say: children promised a prize for drawing later drew about half as much on their own.
  - Must not say: that any reward kills interest (the surprise award did not).
- **Midas (c12).** Ovid, Metamorphoses XI, A. S. Kline's translation (University of Virginia): the twig, stone, earth,
  corn and apple turn to gold, then his bread hardens and the food turns yellow where his teeth touch; his wine is
  molten gold. Wikipedia "Midas" confirms the food and drink, and that the daughter turned to gold is Nathaniel
  Hawthorne's 1852 retelling.
  - What we may say: "in the Greek myth" / "in the old myth", everything he touched turned to gold, even his food.
  - Must not say: the daughter as part of the Greek myth; anything as fact.
- **Groupthink (c13, c14 dropped).** Wikipedia "Groupthink" (Britannica refused the fetch): the desire for harmony
  produces a dysfunctional decision; the word was coined by William H. Whyte Jr. in 1952, and Irving Janis (Yale)
  made it famous in Victims of Groupthink, 1972 (revised 1982). Challenger is cut (c14): Wikipedia's Groupthink page
  uses it as a case, but Diane Vaughan's 1996 book explains it as normalization of deviance.
  - What we may say: psychologists call it groupthink; Irving Janis made the idea famous.
  - Must not say: "Janis coined the word"; Challenger.

## Where the real finding differs from the outline

- Flynn and Lake: "roughly half" is the upper end ("by as much as 50%"), not the average gap. The "twice as many
  people" figure is Study 1 (20.5 predicted vs 10.5 actual); Study 2's was smaller (8.61 vs 5.12 overall).
- Langer: the big request did not just drop; the empty reason fell to exactly the no-reason rate (24%), while a real
  reason still got 42%.
- Milgram: the article does not give run dates; it was received on December 27, 1961, so "run 1961" is safe, "1961-62"
  is true of the whole series (Simply Psychology), not the baseline alone.
- Asch: the overall rate varies by source and paper (32% to 35.7%), so only "about a third" is safe.
- Overjustification: only a promised reward lowered drawing; an unexpected one did not.
- Midas: the golden daughter is Hawthorne (1852), not the Greek myth.
- Groupthink: Janis did not coin the word (Whyte, 1952).

## Looked at, not used

- Cornell eCommons copy of Flynn and Lake: behind a human-verification page; the ILR DigitalCommons copy (same
  manuscript) was used instead.
- PubMed: blocked by a cookie challenge, and the APA papers from 1959-1978 are not abstracted there.
- Britannica (Groupthink, Midas): refused the fetch.
- Semantic Scholar API: rate-limited.
- No primary text of Asch 1951 or 1956 was fetched.
