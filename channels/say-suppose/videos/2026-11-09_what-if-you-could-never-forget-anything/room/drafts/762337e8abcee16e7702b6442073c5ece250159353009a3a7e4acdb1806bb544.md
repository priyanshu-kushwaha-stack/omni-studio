## Premise

It is the night before your board exam and you remember every page you have ever read. You also remember, word for
word, what your mother shouted at you when you were nine, and it is playing right now.
The power is invented. The science is real: people with near-total autobiographical memory exist, they are not
geniuses at exams, and they say forgetting is something they envy.

## The viewer

Riya (`audience.md`), 22, Pune, in final-year exam season; revision at night, chai brought to the desk by a mother
who calls twice a day, a family that argues and makes up. Here "you" sit the board exam, the one every viewer
remembers. Three of five researchers proposed this on their own.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

| Situation | The night before the exam you cram, and say what everyone says: you'll forget it all after anyway |
| Desire | To remember everything, top the exam, never lose a thing you learned |
| Conflict | You keep everything, including every hurt, and treat your mother as the person from one bad day |
| Change | You find forgetting was how you two forgave each other, and that you can still choose what you replay |
| Result | You let one memory go, and take the tea she brings you |

**Research for G2** (not verified yet): Parker, Cahill and McGaugh (2006) and LePort et al. (2012) on highly superior
autobiographical memory; Jill Price's own words about her memory; a source on forgetting as useful (memory research,
not self-help).

**The viewer's "but what about":** Do I remember from before the power? Can I still learn new things? Will I go mad?
Does mom remember it too? Is there a cure?
