| c01 | What you see is already old. Your brain takes about a tenth of a second to process it. | https://elifesciences.org/articles/85301 | it takes the brain about 80–100 milliseconds to process visual information | sourced | eLife Insight, Koevoet, Sahakian & Chota, "How the brain stays in sync with the real world", 19 Jan 2023, on Johnson et al. 2023 (read through the eLife API, same text). "About a tenth of a second" rounds 80–100 ms up; "a fraction of a second" is the safe alternative. The delay is processing across the visual system, not the eyes. Same page: static flashed objects took "about 80 milliseconds"; predictably moving objects were represented near their real current position |
| c02 | There's a trick of the eye called the flash-lag illusion. A light flashes level with a moving dot, but the dot looks ahead of it. | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12200181/ | the flash-lag phenomenon, in which a flashed object appears to lag behind a moving object, when physically the two objects are co-localized at the instant of the flash. | sourced | Nijhawan, "Neural delays, visual motion and the flash-lag effect", Trends Cogn Sci 6(9), 2002 (abstract, read through NCBI E-utilities). Eagleman & Sejnowski 2000 describe it the same way: "a flash and a moving object in the same location appear to be offset" |
| c03 | Some scientists think your brain guesses forward to cover the gap. | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32699152/ | Nijhawan used a perceptual illusion he called the flash-lag effect (FLE) to argue that the brain's visual system solves this computational challenge by extrapolating the position of moving objects (Nijhawan, 1994). | contested | Hogendoorn, J Neurosci 40(30), 2020 (review abstract). Motion extrapolation is one account, and the review argues the evidence now favours it. Rival accounts: postdiction, Eagleman & Sejnowski, Science 287, 2000 (PubMed 10720334): "visual awareness is neither predictive nor online but is postdictive, so that the percept attributed to the time of the flash is a function of events that happen in the approximately 80 milliseconds after the flash"; differential latency, Whitney, Murakami & Cavanagh, Vision Res 40, 2000 (PubMed 10793892): "the apparent flash lag is due to a latency advantage for moving over flashed stimuli", and Patel et al., Science 290, 2000, "Flash-lag effect: differential latency, not postdiction" (PubMed 11184992, title only). Keep "some scientists think"; never say the brain is proven to predict |
| c04 | The fastest balls reach the batter in under half a second. | https://cricketnamibia.com/batting-against-fast-bowling/ | When the ball leaves the bowler’s hand at 150kph (the speed recorded by the radar), it takes 0.48 seconds to arrive at the batsman. | sourced | Units are kilometres per hour. Same page: "it takes 0.2 of a second for the batter to pick up the flight of the ball". Check by sum: 150 km/h is 41.7 m/s; about 20 m from hand to bat gives about 0.48 s. Say "under half a second", not a figure to two decimals |

| c14 | In 1961, a weather scientist, Edward Lorenz, reran a computer weather model with one number rounded, 0.506127 typed in as 0.506, and got a completely different forecast. | https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2003/01/lorenz-butterfly-effect-1961 | Six decimal places were stored in the computer's memory: .506127.
