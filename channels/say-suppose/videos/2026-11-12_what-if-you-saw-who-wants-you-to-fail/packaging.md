## Premise

Whenever you say your good news out loud, or say what you're going for, a stamped paper meter from plus one hundred to minus one hundred flips open above everyone who hears it, for sixty seconds. Plus is glad for you. Minus is not.

**The catch.** It never says why. Pain is pain, and a paper tag has one colour for all of it: the rival who'd enjoy your fall, the friend burning to catch you, and the person who gave up her own life so you could have this one read the same crimson. And it only opens on words said out loud, to a face you can see. A text, a post, a voice down a phone: nothing. So there is always a way to keep someone without ever seeing what you cost them.

**Why this setting.** Seoul, the cram-school streets of Daechi-dong, over one autumn: the six weeks to the college entrance exam, exam day, and results day a month later. The engine of this idea is a win that is structurally someone else's loss, and no place in the ledger builds that into the furniture the way a Korean senior's year does. Your year is the last one graded on the old school curve, where the top school grade goes to the top four per cent (c13): in a classroom that is a handful of seats, and your best friend wants one of them. Then the national exam, where in most subjects the top grade also goes to the top four per cent, but of everyone in the country who sits it (c01): the same word, "four per cent", meaning a seat in one case and a sea in the other, which is the film's late think-moment without any time skip. The real material is unusually filmable and sits at the right hours for a meter over heads: about a thousand cram schools in one neighbourhood (c04), all of them emptying at the same minute because Seoul lets them teach only until ten at night and inspectors have patrolled to enforce it (c03), so the street gives us a thousand meters snapping open at once. Exam day gives the film its scale beat without leaving the season: last year the country grounded flights for thirty-five minutes during the English listening test, the stock market opened an hour late, and police can rush late students to their halls (c02). A country can hold its breath for you; the film asks who else is holding theirs. The brief's second half, a Pangyo startup five years on, a national awards broadcast and a Paris fellowship, is cut: it was the scorer's "time skip rather than the rule escalating", and every one of its jobs (the benign-envy reveal, the sister's counterfactual life, the public moment) can be done inside the season with a notebook, a kimchi jar of brushes and a warehouse loading dock. The alternative place I weighed was Kota, India, which has the same lane and the same streets of coaching signs; it lacks the rule that makes a friend's grade your loss by arithmetic, and it has no day the whole country stops flying. Nothing in the English title or thumbnail is Korean-only: the signboards carry no lettering, and the meter is the only thing written on screen.

**Invented:** you, Min-jun, Ji-yoon, Tae-ho, Ms. Kang, the academy, the scholarship and its letter, every meter and every reading on it, every grade, rank, mock score and "four seats" in the story, the warehouse, the kimchi jar of brushes, the notebook. **Real:** the school curve's top four per cent and the five-tier change after your year (c13); the national exam's nine-grade curve in most subjects, with the top grade for the top four per cent of test-takers (c01); last year's exam day, flights grounded thirty-five minutes, about a hundred and forty rescheduled, the market an hour late, police escorts (c02); cram schools in Seoul teaching only between five in the morning and ten at night, with night patrols (c03); about a thousand cram schools in Daechi-dong (c04); that we measure ourselves against people near our own level (c10) and envy those who started like us in what matters to us, and don't like to admit it, even to ourselves (c12); that when someone close beats you at something central to who you are, closeness sharpens the threat (c06); one brain-scan study in which envying a peer who did better at something that mattered came with activity in a region also active in pain (c05); envy's two kinds, benign that moves you up and malicious that pulls the other down (c07); couples whose partner meets good news with enthusiasm reporting more intimacy and satisfaction (c09); bronze medallists looking happier than silver at the 1992 Olympics (c08); education as the most common life regret across eleven studies, about a third of all regrets, career second (c11).

## Cast

- **You.** Mustard head, teal scarf. Good news, you say out loud, before the ink's dry: Min-jun first, over a split triangle kimbap at ten past ten, then your sister on the phone. You never speak; at most a bubble ("I made it.").
- **Min-jun.** Your best friend since first year, pale-blue head, the next desk. Buys one triangle kimbap at the convenience store every night and holds out half: BUBBLE "Half?" Wants one of the four seats as much as you do. Minus eighty-four.
- **Ji-yoon.** Your older sister, six years older. Peach head, a warehouse vest, night shifts at a logistics depot for six years to pay the academy. Her brushes stand in a kimchi jar on the fridge. Every time you bring news she says "Say it again." The person to lose.
- **Tae-ho.** The rival, sage head, trash-talks you every morning at the shoe lockers. Minus six.
- **Ms. Kang.** The maths teacher, glasses, a red pen. Plus forty-one. Says "Good." and nothing else.
- **The stranger.** Hidden once, in the thousand-tag wide shot at 2:50, reading a timetable at the bus stop. Crimson head, long coat. No line.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: Seoul, the cram-school streets, six weeks before the exam. Good news, you say out loud: Min-jun first, over a split kimbap at ten past ten, then Ji-yoon, who always asks to hear it twice.
> DESIRE: To say it once more and have the face in front of you mean it.
> CONFLICT: The meter shows the hurt your win causes and never what it's about, and the deepest crimson sits over the two people you'd have sworn by.
> CHANGE: You stop reading the number as a wish and start asking what hurts.
> RESULT: You say it to her face, twice, through minus sixty-eight, and you don't let go.
> UNDER: Can someone be glad for you and hurt by you in the same second?
> DARLING: "A tag has one colour for every kind of hurt."
> DARLING: "She was never outside your lane. She was in it first."
> DARLING: "That half-second is the other life, going past."
