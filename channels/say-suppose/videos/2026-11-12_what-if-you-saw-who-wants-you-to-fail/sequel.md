# Sequel: What If Everyone Saw How You Really Felt?

## The hook (the last scene, no new rule)

The kitchen, the morning after results. Two letters on the fridge, six years apart, and the brushes still in the jar. Your best friend at the door with a letter of his own, grinning, and he says it to your face: "I got in!" A paper snap above your own head, out of frame. Your sister's eyes go up, to the air over you. His grin stops, on your face, not on the air above it. You look up, and can't see your own card. We never see the number. The film ends on his face changing. Part 1 planted the question twice without answering it: at the mirror ("So a mirror isn't a face. Or the one card you can't see is yours."), and here. The viewer who comes back for Part 2 remembers one thing: a card opened over you, two people read it, and something in it made your best friend stop smiling.

## The rule twist, one line

The same card, turned round: it opens over your head whenever someone tells you their good news to your face, and everyone in the room can read it but you.

## The opening skit (the owner's note, 2026-11-12)

Part 2 opens the way Part 1 does: a short acted skit before the narrator speaks. The kitchen, the morning after results, from a new angle: over your shoulder. Your best friend at the door, letter up: "I got in!" The snap above you. The camera stays behind your head, so the viewer sees what you see: his grin stopping, your sister's hand going to her mouth, both of them looking at the one place in the room you can't. You turn to the fridge door for your reflection: nothing there, because a mirror never shows a card. Your sister, after a beat: "Say it again." She means him. He does. The second snap. Then the narrator.

## Five beats for Part 2

1. **The door.** His news said out loud, and the snap above you. Your sister reads it from the fridge; her face does the thing his did. Your habit, planted in Part 1, is that you say congratulations first and fastest. The change hits exactly that: now your face says it before your mouth does, and you have no idea what it says. Title question by 0:30: what if everyone could see how you really felt?
2. **Everyone brings you news.** The montage turns at every use. The rival tells you his score just to watch the card; your maths teacher says "Good" about her own daughter and glances up; the clerk tells you the shop is his now and reads you while he scans; the kid from the bus, back on the bus, tells you he lost a tooth and claps at whatever opens over you. People start saying things to your face to read you, and the whole class trades numbers you never saw. The gap every time: you expect your own face to be plus one hundred, and the room's eyes say it isn't.
3. **The limit, mirrored.** Only out loud, only to a face: so you start taking news by text, and asking people to call. The cards go quiet because you've stopped letting anyone look. Your best friend notices first: he stops telling you things, and stops holding out the half. The loophole from Part 1, the screen you could keep her on, is now the screen you keep yourself behind. Open loop: the person who can't see your number is the only one who still wants to.
4. **Her news.** The cost, at 60%. Your sister, with the scholarship money freeing her nights, takes the brushes out of the jar and says it to your face: she's applied to the art school, and she's in. The snap above your head. She reads it, and her arms stay up. You can't see it, and you can't ask, because asking is admitting there's something to read. Dilemma at 75%: ask her what she saw, and live with the answer; or never ask, and keep the hug you think you still have.
5. **Say it again.** The pay-off is the gag turned round. On the loading dock at six, you ask her to say it again, to your face, and you let her read you twice. The second card opens on whatever the first one said, and this time she says the number out loud, and what she thinks it's about, and she's wrong about the why too. A card has one colour for every kind of hurt, and so does a face. The residue, inside the last image of the empty jar: tomorrow someone will tell you their good news, and for half a second your face will answer before you do, and they'll see it, and stay anyway.
