# Packaging: saw who uses AI to text you

status: G1 draft, round 1 (2026-11-13). Written by Claude Fable 5.1 (`claude -p`)

## Premise

On your birthday, a small red dot appears beside every message on your phone that a machine wrote and a person only pressed send on, or didn't even do that. Only you can see the dots, and they are never wrong.

**The catch.** A dot says who wrote it. It never says why. The same crimson sits on the landlord's rent reminder, on the man who has flirted with you for a month without typing a word, and on the paragraph from your best friend that made you cry, and the dot cannot tell you that one of those three was written by someone exhausted who told a machine never to let you go unanswered. And your own messages get dots too. You have been letting the phone finish your sentences for years, and you had forgotten.

**Why this setting.** Manila, the Philippines, over about two weeks around your birthday. The idea needs a place where a message is not a convenience but the relationship itself, so that a dot on it costs something. No place in the ledger builds that into the furniture like this one. Around the turn of the century the Philippines was called the texting capital of the world (c01): about thirty million texts a day in 2001, twice all of Europe, over a billion a day by 2008 (c02). About two million Filipinos work overseas (c04), so for millions of families the phone is where the family happens, and Filipinos are among the heaviest social-media users anywhere, with the "big family group chat" the place the family keeps itself (c05). That gives the film its cast without invention: a father eight months out on a cargo ship whose love arrives as three misspelt words; a mother who runs a group chat of forty cousins on three continents; aunts whose machines write poems to each other; a best friend on a night shift. Two corrections from research hold throughout: the nickname belongs to the country, not the city, and it is "was called", never "is"; the brief's "hundreds of millions a day" is dropped for the sourced figures. The alternative I weighed was the United States, where every study in the ledger was run (c20, c21, c22) and where the polls on AI-written texts live (c23). It lost because there a text is one channel among many; here, for the two million, it is the only one, and the dark-city image at the peak means more in a country that once sent a billion a day. The setting stays in the pictures and never in the words: a kitchen, a bus, a call centre, a corner cafe, a ship; no local term, food or place name anywhere in the telling, and nothing in the English title or thumbnail reads one country only.

**Invented:** you, the dot and its rule, your best friend and her night shift, your father's ship, your mother's shoebox of cards, the two aunts, the man from the dating app, your boss and the offer, every message, every number inside the story (forty-one messages, thirty-three dots, twenty-four years of cards, a million signatures and four hundred people), the cafe neither of you booked, the typo as a signature. **Real, and spoken:** the Philippines once called the texting capital of the world (c01); over a billion texts a day by 2008 (c02); about two million Filipinos working overseas (c04); a study this year in which people told a personal message was written by AI called the sender lazy and insincere, while people told nothing mostly never suspected (c20); Gmail offering to write replies since 2015 and to finish sentences since 2018 (c11); Hallmark printing greeting cards since 1915, so people have signed their names under someone else's words for over a hundred years (c14); Dunbar's layers, about five very close people and about a hundred and fifty in all, limited by time (c19); one security company's estimate that in 2024 bots passed people at fifty-one per cent of web traffic, for the first time in a decade (c09); the 2023 study in which people who suspected AI-suggested replies judged their partner more harshly, even though using them made the chat warmer (c22). **Real, as the writer's lenses, never spoken:** the effort heuristic (c12), Zahavi's costly signal (c13), Cyrano (c16). The big family group chat (c05) is the picture's support, not a spoken statistic.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: Your birthday in Manila, your father eight months out on a ship, forty-one messages before breakfast, and you grading every one by its length: long means love.
> DESIRE: To know who, of everyone who texts you, actually means it.
> CONFLICT: The dot tells you who wrote a message and never why, and it sits on the warmest messages on your phone, your best friend's first, then your own.
> CHANGE: You stop reading a dot as "didn't care" and go and look at the person behind it.
> RESULT: You send your father three words with a typo in them, and leave the typo.
> UNDER: If a machine wrote it but they meant it, does it still count?
> DARLING: "A dot says who wrote it. It never says why."
> DARLING: "She was keeping you with the only hours she had left: the ones she was asleep for."
> DARLING: "Nobody wrote it. Both of you meant it."

**How it differs from the nearest video.** Ifverse-style "What If You Could Hear Everyone's Thoughts" and the channel's own deleted-texts and who-cares pitches reveal hidden feelings, and the reveal is the reward. Ours reveals hidden authorship, which is happening for real right now (c20, c22), and the reveal is the wound at 0:24. The film is about being wrong to read it as one. "What If You Saw Who Wants You to Fail?" measured what your good news does to the people who hear it; this one measures nothing about feeling at all, only who typed, and its turn is that the machine-written message can carry more care than the human one, and that the best conversation of your year had no human in it. No other what-if ends on a typo left in.
