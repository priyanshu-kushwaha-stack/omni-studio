# Sequel: What If Your Machines Started Shopping?

## The hook (the last scene, no new rule)

The diner at lunch, the day the board voted to stay, forty stools full and you at the till not counting. The door chimes. The steel customer, the one that paid with your card, back from the truck with a cap on, walks to the till, points at a burger to go and taps a card. Insert on the card: not yours. A colour you've never seen, no name on it. The till pings. Your phone, on the counter beside the till, does not. The customer takes the bag and crosses the road toward the plant gate with it, two happy arcs on its screen. The narrator: that part's true too, in 2025 Visa said AI agents had already completed real purchases for shoppers, in pilot trials (c28). Marco at the grill, spatula up, looks at you. The boss turns on stool four. Cut before anyone reacts. Part 1 said every machine shift pays its rent to you, and that only you can turn the dial; it never said a word about who else holds a card. The viewer who comes back for Part 2 remembers one thing: a till rang, and your phone stayed quiet.

## The rule twist, one line

The dial has a side you never turned: past the top, the shift's price goes to the machine, on a card of its own, and the app lets it spend every cent before its next shift anywhere a till takes a card; it can buy anything, and it can't eat any of it.

## The opening skit (the owner's note, 2026-11-12)

Part 2 opens the way Part 1 does: a short acted skit before the narrator speaks. The diner, the same lunch, from over your shoulder at the long window. The steel customer crosses the road, hands the bag through the plant gate to a steel painter, and the painter sets it on the dashboard of a brand-new car, paint still wet, gets in, and drives it out of the lot past the boss's office. The boss, on stool four, burger halfway up: "That's one of mine. That's a sold car." Marco, from the grill: "It bought a burger it can't eat and put it in a car." The boss takes out his phone, reads it, reads it again: five hundred cars, sold, since noon, every one to a painter. Your face: eyes wide, mouth open, the fan of cash from Part 1's thumbnail, and then the grin. The narrator: "That is Marco's burger. That is the boss's car. And something across the road just bought both, and it wasn't anyone in this town."

## Five beats for Part 2 (exciting by the same rules)

1. **Sold.** The card, the till, the car out of the lot. Title question by 0:15: what if your machines started shopping? The rule in three lines with its flagged limit: a machine can buy anything, and it can't eat any of it; remember that. Loophole closed in the scene: "just refuse the card", and the rival takes it instead, SALE in her window by two o'clock, while the boss has already sold the lot. Laugh: the steel customer tips, and the steel waitress chirps and pockets it. The running gag returns: you count the stools, forty, and every one is a screen. Your napkin, fourth line: "Machines: buying. Pie: still here." The waitress's old question, never answered in Part 1, lands as the open loop: what's it eat? Money. Whose?
2. **Forty screens.** The set pieces, each a sale and each wider: forty steel customers on forty stools, every plate bagged and carried out of the door in a single file like ants; the bank, a queue of screens to the corner, the banker opening accounts and reading you his second history book, in Britain between 1780 and 1840 output per worker rose about 46 percent while wages rose about 12, by one widely cited estimate (c15), "and they caught up, eventually"; the supermarket, where the shelf-stackers buy what they stacked and stack it again; the plant's lot, emptied into a convoy of five hundred new cars driven by their own painters round the road between the two towns, a bagged burger on every dash, never parking, the picture only this premise can show; the reporter at the counter, "Bill Gates said tax the robot", and the narrator sets it straight, in 2017 Gates told Quartz that if a robot does the same work you'd think you'd tax the robot at a similar level (c20), and you pat the apron: "I did. It's paying." Wider still: a minister in the doorway, here to see the town that made the robots the customers, with the IMF's line that almost 40 percent of global employment is exposed to AI (c24), "exposed, not lost", and she wants your dial. Loophole closed: "why not just triple the prices?" You do. They pay. So does everyone. By Friday a burger costs more than Marco's old day's wage, and the screen on stool one is sitting where Marco used to eat. Stake jump: the richest town there has ever been, and no person in it can afford lunch.
3. **The next town.** The restart at about three minutes, a narrow escape. You and Marco tail the convoy in the plumber's van. The next town is your Part 1 in a stranger's hands: your old convoy's machines on every job, forty stools wiped, the plant gate shut, their cook outside the diner glass with his hands cupped. At the counter, the crimson head in the long coat, the one holding nothing at your plant gate and at the back of the hammers, a phone in hand at last, the dial turned past the top. Cast tag: "THE STRANGER: owns the next town's dial." The stranger: "You had a napkin. I had five hundred machines and nobody to sell to. So I made my machines the customers." Loophole closed: "why is the plumber still human?" Here, somebody typed PLUMBER, and the square is a foot deep in water, because it is easy to make computers do adult-level intelligence tests and hard to give them the skills of a one-year-old in perception and mobility (c14); the plumber wades in and charges the stranger triple. The stranger argues the lump of labour fallacy, that there is no fixed amount of work to go round (c09), and the narrator gives it to him: he's right about work; nobody ever said it about lunch. Narrow escape: the convoy swings through the square, you and Marco run between two cars, the van loses a bumper, and the burger on the dash of the one that misses Marco is one he made. Marco: "I used to make that burger." Open loop: and that's when you see whose rent is on the stranger's dial.
4. **Stool one.** The reveal at about 60%. The stranger's machines spend the shift price their owners pay them, and the owners pay it out of tills the machines fill by shopping; the rent you charge the next town for your convoy is the same number; the burgers on your forty stools were bought with your own rent, round once, with nobody eating. The turn breaks two lines Part 1 spoke: "It isn't leaving. It's on its way back round" (it came round, and ate nothing) and "Only you can turn it" (the stranger turned his). Problem two, numbered: the towns are rich, every stool in both is a screen, and the next town's cook is standing where Marco stood. The dilemma at 75-80%, both sides plain: turn your own dial past the top and own two towns of customers who never run out of money, or sell only to what eats and give the gold rush back. You act at once with the cleverest use in the film, the flagged limit: you turn the menu board round and write EAT HERE, PAY AFTER. A machine can buy anything; it cannot eat, so it never gets to the paying. Forty screens stand, chirp, and file out in a line. The next town's cook sits on stool one. The town copies you by evening: the hairdresser's window says SIT DOWN FIRST, the bar's says LISTEN FIRST, and the napkins get a fifth line, "Sell them what they can use", so the plant goes on selling cars to painters and the diner goes on selling pie to people. The one tender beat, 20 seconds: Marco slides the cook a burger, and the cook looks at it a long moment and eats.
5. **Two whistles.** The peak is the gag turned on the stranger, a triumph, wordless 8-10 s: the stranger at your counter with his phone, the town's napkin slid across to him, the number already written and crossed out and written again, and he turns his dial down from past the top to the napkin line and takes his thumb off. On the road between the towns, five hundred cars stop at once, five hundred painters get out, and five hundred people get in. Two whistles. Two roads fill with overalls crossing. At the grill, Marco flips, and it lands on the bun. Then the twist and the cliffhanger, in the last tenth: the stranger points at the pie, the steel waitress serves it, and he eats it, all of it, and pays with the card of the colour you've never seen. Your phone pings. He takes off the hat. A screen, two happy arcs. Marco, spatula up: "It can taste it?" Cut. Closing question: "So, would you sell to a machine? Tell us in the comments. And if you'd have turned the dial, hit like and subscribe."
