# Sequel: What If Your Debt Doubled Every Night?

**The hook.** The last shot of the first film: Jonas, your flatmate, pays for both rolls, glances at his own phone, and his face changes. We never saw the screen. This film opens on it. Minus one cent. The bank charged him a cent for the receipt he asked for on day eleven, the morning you first paid for your own roll. He laughs. He is the man who always pays. He will pay this too.

**The rule twist.** The same rule, the other side of zero. Whatever Jonas owes at midnight doubles by morning. The only stop is still exactly zero, but a debt cannot be given away, spent or erased by the bank: a debt is not a mistake, as the man from the bank says on day nine. The only way to zero is for someone to pay it for him, exactly, before midnight. And Jonas has never in his life let anyone pay. The money your doubling made thin now comes back the other way: as his debt grows, the street's money gets scarce, and Lotte's board goes down instead of up, to one ten, to seventy cents, until the flour man will not sell flour at any price she can charge. Lotte loses both ways. The under-question: if you never let anyone pay for you, who do you belong to?

**Five beats.**

1. **Minus one cent (days one to twelve).** Jonas treats it as a joke and pays it off every morning: two cents, four, a euro, ten. He never tells you. You find out on day twelve, when he pays for your roll with a face you have never seen on him, and the board says one twenty. Down. Lotte notices before you do.
2. **The man who always pays (days thirteen to twenty-five).** He sells the flask, the records, the club you bought him. The fans buy the club back and give it to him. He will not take it. Every evening he pays what he can, and every midnight the rest doubles. The bank man: a debt is not a mistake. The street's prices keep falling, which looks like good news until the flour man stops coming.
3. **Lotte's board goes down (days twenty-six to forty).** Seventy cents a roll and no one has seventy cents. Lotte bakes half, then a quarter. The pensioners come back and she gives the rolls away. Jonas's debt passes what the whole street earns in a year, then the city. He stops coming down to the counter. You, with exactly nothing since the night you erased it, go up the stairs with two rolls Lotte would not let you pay for.
4. **The dilemma (day fifty-two, about 78%).** The debt can be paid only by someone who is owed nothing by Jonas, exactly, at midnight. Lotte has one thing: the cent you gave her, which she never put in the till, and a bakery she could sign over. Jonas can let the person he has carried for three years carry him, once, or keep paying until the street has no money left to pay with. He says "Not me" one more time, and this time it is the wrong answer.
5. **Somebody pays (day fifty-two, 23:50 to the next morning).** Through the glass, Lotte's "Counted?" to Jonas, for the first time. He opens his hand. You count: Lotte's cent, the building's deed, and your own two cents from a refund that arrived that afternoon, to the exact figure, into the bank man's form. Zero at midnight. Zero, doubled, is still zero. Morning: the board says one forty, in white chalk. Jonas pays for your roll, and you let him, and he lets Lotte slide him a day-old roll, free, which he has never once allowed. The residue: a receipt is somebody saying you were here. The close: "So, would you have let them pay? Tell us in the comments. And if you'd have done the same, hit like and subscribe."
