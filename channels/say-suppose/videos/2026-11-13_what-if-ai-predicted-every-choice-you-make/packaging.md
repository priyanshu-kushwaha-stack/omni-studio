## Premise

One evening a city worker clips a small teal lamp to the lamppost over your food court, and a stamped paper tag drops on a thread over every head, yours included: what that person will do in the next ten seconds, and how sure the city is. Do what your tag says and everything you buy gets cheaper, your coffee tonight, your rent by the month. Break it and the tag goes crimson where everyone can see, and your prices climb. The city is right ninety-nine times in a hundred, about everybody, and within a week being boring is the best-paid job in town.

**The catch.** The discount buys your surprises. The cheapest people in the city are the ones who never do anything new, so nobody does: the joke dies before it's told, the date reads her tag instead of his face, a whole train looks at its phones because the fare drops when it does. And a crimson tag is a verdict with no trial. Your best friend, who runs the stall with you and has held out his hand for the cash-box key every night for years as a joke, gets a reading one night that is no joke: TAKES THE KEY, then, a week later, with the month's rent in the box, TAKES THE BOX, ninety-four per cent, and the whole food court can see it over his head. Your own tag says you'll pocket the key. The city will pay you to.

**The turn underneath.** Real machines are bad at people: a brain scan guesses a two-way button press at about sixty per cent (c04, c05), and 160 teams with thousands of facts per family barely beat four facts at predicting how children's lives turn out (c24). So the city's ninety-nine never came from seeing further. It came from the discount. Tracked drivers braked hard about a fifth less once a discount rode on it (c17); lean on a number to control people and the number stops behaving (c18). The machine was right because the city bent to it, and the one thing it has never seen is a choice nobody has made before. That is the line the film plants at 0:45 and breaks at 8:15, and it is the only way out at the counter.

**Why this setting.** Singapore, a food court and its train, over about five weeks of one season, in dollars. The engine of this idea is a city that puts a price over your head for what you choose to do, and only one place in the ledger has done that in the real world: since 1998 Singapore has charged drivers electronically for its busiest roads, by road, traffic and time of day, the first scheme of its kind anywhere (c30). A gantry over the road that bills you for where you went and when is the film's rule with one word changed. In 2017 the same city said it aimed to bring every one of its hundred and ten thousand lampposts into a sensor network (c30), so the skit's first picture, a worker on a ladder clipping a lamp to a lamppost, is the real plan drawn in paper. I weighed two alternatives. An American city: the ledger's richest seam is there, the insurer that cuts your price if it can watch you drive (c16, c17), the life insurer that rewards tracked habits (c29), the court score (c21, c22). But each is a company's product or a courtroom's, with no picture over anyone's head and no city behind it, and every one of those facts is spoken just as well from a food court on the other side of the world, which the film does. And no place at all, a generic near-future city: it keeps the tags and loses the gantry and the lampposts, the one real floor under "a city that already prices where you go". Singapore wins on one row and one lamppost. Plain words still rule: the narration says "the food court", "the stall", "iced coffee", "the train", "the landlord"; the city is named once, in the narrator's first line, and no story detail needs a local word or name to follow.
