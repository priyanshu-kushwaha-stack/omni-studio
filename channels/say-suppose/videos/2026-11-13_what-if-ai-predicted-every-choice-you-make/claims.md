# Claims ledger: What If AI Predicted Every Choice You Make? (G1)

Started at G1 (2026-11-13) so the claims check can read the sources before any packaging states a fact; G2 adds a row
for every real-world claim the script speaks. Every source was fetched and read on 2026-11-13 by three research passes
(brain and randomness; prediction in commerce; incentives and feedback loops). Quotes are verbatim from the fetched
text. Where the primary page was blocked, the note says where the words were read (a reprint, the authors' press
release, an open-access copy, or a reliable secondary source).

The story is invented and carries no row: the city's forecast tags and every reading on them, the discount, the
"confirm rate", the penalties, you, your best friend and everyone else in the story, every percentage the story's
machine shows. What the machine can do is the story's rule, not a claim about any real system; the film says what real
studies found only where a row below supports it.

## Dropped or corrected from the topic brief

- **Libet 1983, "350 to 500 ms before a person consciously feels the urge"**: corrected. The brain signal starts about
  550 ms before the movement and the reported urge about 200 ms before it, a gap of about 350 ms on spontaneous acts
  (c01, c02). The act was a wrist flick with no choice of what to do, only when. Libet himself argued for a veto (c03).
- **Soon et al. 2008, "predict left or right up to 7 to 10 seconds before"**: corrected. A two-way button choice was
  decodable up to 7 s before the reported decision in the fMRI signal, up to 10 s after correcting for its lag, at about
  60% against 50% chance (c04, c05). Never "your brain decides ten seconds before you do".
- **Barabási 2010, "proved that daily routines are 93% predictable"**: corrected. 93% is a theoretical upper bound on
  predicting a person's location (by cell tower, by hour), from 50,000 phone users over three months; no actual
  predictor hit it (c09).
- **Transformers "assign exact percentage weights to the next action"**: dropped (padding; the scorer's note).
- **Markov chains, "a single out-of-distribution action resets the downstream probability tree"**: dropped (padding;
  not a sourced finding).
- **Pupillometry, "pupil dilation telegraphs a decision 1 to 2 seconds before speech"**: dropped (not checked; padding).
- **Telematics, "Progressive Snapshot and Tesla Insurance adjust premiums in real time"**: replaced by c16 and c17
  (Progressive's own page; the 21% hard-braking study). Tesla not checked; not used.
- **COMPAS, "demonstrating the mathematical impossibility..."**: split into what each source says: ProPublica's false
  flags (c21), Dressel and Farid's crowd (c22), Kleinberg's proof (c23).
- **Bayes, "95% sensitivity and specificity yields a high fraction of false positives on rare events"**: true as
  arithmetic, but no source was fetched; not used at G1. If the script needs the base-rate point, G2 adds a sourced row.
- **Singapore, "over 110,000 police and civic sensors"**: corrected. A former head of the civil service said in 2017
  the country aimed to bring all 110,000 lampposts into a sensor network (c30); no source says it was done.
- **Goodhart 1975, "when a measure becomes a target..."**: that sentence is Marilyn Strathern's (1997), not
  Goodhart's. Goodhart's own words are in c18; the short form, credited correctly, is c19.

Also not used: the Target father in Minneapolis as fact (c13 is contested: one unnamed employee, never confirmed);
the "87%" in the Target article (a made-up example shopper); "Amazon ships things before you order them" (c14 is a
patent, use never shown); "Uber charges more when your battery is low" (its economist said it does not, c15); the
Delhi cobra bounty (probably a myth; Hanoi, c20, is archival); a win rate for the New York Times rock-paper-scissors
bot (no readable source gives one); Wagenaar 1972 as proof that "humans can't be random" (his own conclusion was
that results were not conclusive); "win-stay lose-shift" as what everyone plays (c08 says winners tend to stay; the
losers' shift held only under some payoffs); Kosinski 2013's "85% of cases" for politics (a pairwise measure, not
checked); Soleymanian's sample size; Smart Compose's "1 billion" figure; and the Lum and Isaac Oakland figure as a
real deployment (c26 is a simulation).

## For the writer (research notes, not claims)

- **The twist has real ground.** Tracked drivers changed how they braked once a discount rode on it (c17); an insurer
  calls its own programme "a behavior change platform" (c29); a measure that becomes a target stops measuring (c18,
  c19; Hanoi's rat farms, c20); a prediction that drives action is "performative" (c25) and can feed itself (c26);
  Russell argues feeds win by making people more predictable (c27); Merton's prophet cites what happened as proof
  (c28), and "Halley's comet" is the clean contrast: predicting a comet does not move it, predicting a person does.
- **The machine is weaker than it looks.** Brain-scan "prediction" is about 60% on a two-way choice (c04, c05), and
  the early signal vanishes when the choice matters (c06); 160 teams with 12,942 facts per family barely beat four
  facts (c24); a crowd of strangers matched a court's software (c22). So the film's scary 99% has to come from people
  getting simpler, not machines getting smarter. That is the twist, and it is true to the evidence.
- **The skit material is real.** The f/d key program (c07) and rock-paper-scissors habits (c08) are an acted scene
  waiting to happen: you try to be random and lose to a tiny program.
- **Recommenders are the everyday hook.** Netflix about 80% (c12), YouTube over 70% (c12), the Netflix Prize tie by
  twenty minutes (c11), Target's 25 products and the lawn mower next to the diapers (c13).
- **World.** Singapore's real road pricing (c30) suits a city that prices behaviour, but the owner's plain-words rule
  means no "hawker", "teh peng" or local names in the telling. Whether Singapore stays is a Phase B call.
