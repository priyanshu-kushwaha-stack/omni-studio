# Research: free will, brain prediction, human (non-)randomness

Film: "What If AI Predicted Every Choice You Make?" (Say Suppose). Fetch date for every row: **2026-11-13**.

How the quotes were read:
- **Abstracts** come as raw text from the Europe PMC REST API (`EXT_ID:<PMID>` or `DOI:` query). They are verbatim.
- **Soon et al. 2008 full text** is from the authors' manuscript PDF on Charité's site, with text pulled locally. The wording can differ slightly from the published Nature Neuroscience version; for example, the manuscript abstract says "even up to ten seconds" and the published one says "up to 10 s".
- **Wagenaar 1972, Song 2010, Wang 2014 and Lages & Jaworska 2012** were read in full (PDF or Europe PMC XML) and the quotes taken from that text.
- **Rows marked "via WebFetch"** were quoted by the fetch tool from the live page in short pieces; I did not see the raw page.
- **Not reachable:** nytimes.com (blocked) and pnas.org (403). PMC pages sometimes served a CAPTCHA, which I did not attempt.

| In 1983, Benjamin Libet found that brain activity building up to a simple wrist flick started a few hundred milliseconds before people said they felt the urge to move. | https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/106.3.623 (abstract via Europe PMC, PMID 6640273) | "The onset of cerebral activity clearly preceded by at least several hundred milliseconds the reported time of conscious intention to act." … "In series with type II RPs, onset of the main negative shift in each RP preceded the corresponding mean W value by an average of about 350 ms, and by a minimum of about 150 ms." | sourced | **5 subjects**, at least 6 sessions each, 40 movements per series. The **350 ms** gap is between RP onset and the reported urge (W), on spontaneous series. When some preplanning was reported, the gap was **~800 ms** (or 500 ms by another onset measure). W was **self-reported** from the remembered clock position of a revolving spot. The act was a meaningless flick with no choice of *what* to do, only *when*. |
| Put on a timeline: the brain signal starts about half a second before the movement, and the felt urge comes about a fifth of a second before it. | https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00044903 (Libet 1985, BBS 8:529, abstract; via WebFetch from cambridge.org) | "the main negative RP shift begins at about—550 ms." … "W occurred at about—200 ms." | sourced | These are **–550 ms (RP onset)** and **–200 ms (W)** relative to movement, for spontaneous acts with no preplanning. | A 2012 study suggested the "readiness" signal may be random brain noise drifting up to a threshold, not a decision already made. | https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1210467109 (Schurger, Sitt & Dehaene 2012, abstract via Europe PMC) | "the precise moment at which the decision threshold is crossed leading to movement is largely determined by spontaneous subthreshold fluctuations in neuronal activity. The full text is paywalled; I read only the abstract. |
| When people made a real choice (where $1,000 of charity money went), the readiness signal disappeared. | https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.39787 (Maoz, Yaffe, Koch & Mudrik 2019, eLife; abstract via Europe PMC) | "While we found the expected RPs for arbitrary decisions, they were strikingly absent for deliberate ones." | sourced | Strong counterweight for the film. The famous signals come from **arbitrary** choices (which finger, when to flick). The authors call those choices "purposeless, unreasoned, and without consequences". |
| In 2008, brain scans could guess whether you'd press the left or right button seconds before you felt you'd chosen, but only a bit better than a coin flip. | https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2112 (Soon, Brass, Heinze & Haynes 2008); manuscript: https://www.charite.de/fileadmin/user_upload/portal/charite/presse/pressemitteilungen/2008/Haynes_UnconsciousDeterminants_NN2008.pdf | Abstract (published): "We found that the outcome of a decision can be encoded in brain activity of prefrontal and parietal cortex up to 10 s before it enters awareness." ## (b) Do not say

1. **"Scientists proved free will is an illusion."** No study here shows that. Libet himself proposed a conscious **veto** (~150 ms window). Schultze-Kraft 2016 found people can cancel up to **~200 ms** before moving. Schurger 2012 and Maoz 2019 suggest the readiness potential may be accumulated noise that appears only for **arbitrary** choices.
2. **"Your brain decides 10 seconds before you do."** Soon 2008 was a **binary left/right button** choice, decoded at **~60%** (chance 50%), and "**up to**" 7 s in the fMRI signal or 10 s after the blood-flow correction. Say "brain scans could guess a simple left-or-right choice a little better than a coin flip, seconds early".
3. **"Brain scans can predict your choices with high accuracy."** Before the decision, accuracy is ~59–64%. A classifier using only the person's **previous answers** did about as well (61.6–64.1%; Lages & Jaworska 2012). The >80% figure (Fried 2011) is for **whether/when** a movement comes, from implanted electrodes, 0.7 s ahead.
4. **"Libet found the brain decides half a second before you're aware."** The numbers are **~550 ms** RP onset and **~200 ms** felt urge before movement, so the gap is **~350 ms**. W is a **self-report** read off a clock. The "decision" was **when** to flick a wrist, with no stakes.
5. **"An algorithm predicted where people would be 93% of the time."** 93% is a **theoretical maximum** from entropy, not the hit rate of any predictor. It is location at the **cell-tower and hour** level, from **50,000** (analyzed 45,000) users over **3 months**.
6. **"Facebook knows you better than your spouse."** The computer **matched** the spouse at the average Like count (r 0.56 vs 0.58). The model needed about **300 Likes** to beat a spouse, and the measure was agreement with a **personality questionnaire**, not predicting choices.
7. **"The NYT rock-paper-scissors bot beats humans X% of the time."** No readable source gives a win rate, and a reporter beat it in 20 rounds.
8. **"People play win-stay, lose-shift."** Winners do tend to repeat. Losers shift in a consistent direction only under some payoff settings (a ≥ 2), and the fitted strategy "differ[s] considerably" from pure win-stay/lose-shift.
9. **"Aaronson's study showed…"** It was a **classroom demo** described in lecture notes, with no sample size. Present it as "a professor's simple program", and say it guessed **70–80%** of keypresses.
10. **"Humans can never be random" as a settled law (Wagenaar).** Wagenaar found "almost all" experiments showed systematic deviations, mostly **too many alternations**. His own conclusion was that the results were "not … conclusive" because methods varied.
