## (b) Do not say

- "Goodhart said: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." That wording is Strathern's (1997). Goodhart's own is the "statistical regularity will tend to collapse" line.
- "In colonial Delhi, people bred cobras for the bounty." Probably a myth with no primary record. Use Hanoi 1902 (archival, Vann 2003).
- "Tracked drivers were 21% safer" / "21% fewer crashes." It was a 21% drop in daily hard-brake events after six months, in one insurer's self-selected customers.
- "Progressive raises most people's rates." Its page says about 2 in 10 get an increase.
- "John Hancock forces customers to wear fitness trackers." The Vitality programme came with every policy; logging activity was optional.
- "COMPAS labels Black people high risk twice as often." ProPublica's figure is about being *wrongly* flagged (false positives): "almost twice the rate".
- "A random stranger beats the algorithm." The pooled crowd matched it (67.0% vs 65.2%); the median single person was slightly worse (62.8%).
- "Oakland police used AI that targeted Black people twice as often." Lum & Isaac ran a simulation on Oakland data; it was not a deployment.
- "Studies prove recommender algorithms change your preferences." Russell argues this; Chaney et al. show homogenisation in simulation. Neither is a field measurement on real users.
- "Singapore's road prices change in real time" or "every Singapore lamppost has a face-recognition camera." LTA says rates "are regularly reviewed" (a search summary of LTA's OneMotoring page said quarterly reviews and half-hourly steps at peak; not fetched, unverified); the lamppost network was a stated aim and a pilot.
- "Hawker culture is a UNESCO World Heritage Site." It is on the intangible heritage list (2020).
- Any number for the Soleymanian sample size (e.g. "100,000 drivers over 32 months"): seen only in a search summary, not verified.
