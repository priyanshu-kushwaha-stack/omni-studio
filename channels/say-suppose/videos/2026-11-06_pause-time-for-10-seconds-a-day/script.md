# Script: What If You Could Pause Time for 10 Seconds a Day?

format: story
status: draft v5 for G2 (review rounds 1 to 4 applied: review-g2-round1.md to review-g2-round4.md)
claims: claims.md

## [00:00] The change
> SCENE: Flash-forward, two seconds. A grey, frozen street. A bus a metre from a school-age kid on a crossing. The split-flap counter reads 7. Cut to black.
On day twelve, all that stands between a bus and a kid is ten seconds.
> SCENE: Bedroom, morning. The alarm clock rings. You snap your fingers at it, and the room drains to grey from your hand outward. Dust hangs in the light.
But on day one, you just wake up and discover you can pause time.
The alarm's frozen mid-ring. The dust hangs in the air. Your cat's stuck halfway off the bed.
So what if you could pause time for ten seconds a day?
> SCENE: Colour floods back. The cat lands. The alarm finishes its ring.

## [00:20] The rule
> SCENE: The split-flap counter drops in, top right, reading 10. You snap; the bedroom goes grey.
Ten seconds. Once a day. Everyone and everything stops. Except you.
> SCENE: The counter flips down to 0. Colour snaps back; the cat finishes its jump.
When the ten run out, the world starts again, and nobody knows.
> SCENE: A bedside clock turns to midnight. The counter's flaps blank, then flip back to 10.
And unused seconds vanish at midnight.
> SCENE: Grey again. A translucent amber block snaps shut around you. You push against it, stuck.
> SCENE: Black frame. Only your two eyes, blinking.
Real physics would wreck this power. Stopped air would seal you in like a fly in glass, and stopped light couldn't reach your eyes (c01, c02).
> SCENE: The block shatters into paper flakes. Colour returns.
So we cheat. Air moves for you. You can see. And anything you touch moves with you, then stops dead when you let go.
> SCENE: You hold up one finger, thinking.
So, what's your first pause for?
> SCENE: You glance at the coffee machine and grin.
Coffee. Obviously.

## [00:50] The first days
> SCENE: Kitchen. Your elbow knocks the mug off the counter.
> TAG: 0.45 S
A mug falls a metre in about 0.45 seconds (c04). Usually, you just watch it go.
Not today.
> SCENE: You snap. Grey. The mug hangs in mid-air, the coffee frozen in droplets.
Pause.
You stroll over. There's no rush. At an ordinary walking pace, ten seconds covers about 10 to 14 metres (c05).
> TAG: 10–14 M
> SCENE: You pluck the mug out of the air, turn it upright and scoop the droplets back in. A smirk.
You catch your falling coffee.
> SCENE: Colour returns. You sip.
Ten seconds is more time than it sounds.
> SCENE: Hallway at work. Your boss steps out of an office, waving.
> BUBBLE: Boss: "Got a minu—"
Your boss spots you in the hallway. Stop and chat, or vanish?
> SCENE: You snap. Grey. The boss frozen mid-wave. You tiptoe past, under his arm.
Vanish.
> SCENE: Colour. The boss blinks at the empty hallway.
> SCENE: Office kitchen. One samosa left on the plate. A colleague's hand reaches for it.
The last samosa in the office kitchen. A colleague's hand is already reaching. Do you let it go?
> SCENE: You snap. Grey. You swap the samosa for a napkin and stroll off chewing. Colour. The colleague stares at the napkin.
Of course not. You're not a saint. You're a person with ten seconds.
> SCENE: Home. Your mother's chappal flies across the room at your head. You snap. Grey. It hangs mid-air. You step one pace left. Colour. It sails past. She stares at her hand.
Your mother's chappal, mid-air, headed for your head? You step aside. She never works out how she missed.
> SCENE: Close on the hallway wall clock. Your eyes dart to it. The second hand sticks for a beat, then jumps.
Here's the strange part. You've felt a tiny pause before.
Have you ever glanced at a clock and the second hand seemed to stick for a moment?
> TAG: CHRONOSTASIS
That's real. Scientists call it chronostasis (c09).
It happens every time your eyes jump. You only notice when there's a clock to catch it (c10).
> SCENE: A paper TV remote drops into your hand.
Your brain has been editing time for you all your life. Now you've got the remote.

## [02:30] The first year
> TAG: DAY 12
Then comes day twelve. You're walking home.
> SCENE: Street, afternoon. Crowds, pigeons, a zebra crossing. A bus comes down the road.
A bus runs a red light.
> SCENE: A school-age kid in a blue cap chases a ball onto the crossing.
There's a kid in the road.
Do you shout? Do you run?
Neither works. Not in time.
> SCENE: Far back in the crowd, a figure in a long dark coat, with a crimson head, stands still, facing the wrong way. Nobody looks at them.
You hit pause.
> SCENE: The world drains to grey from you outward. The bus stops a metre from the kid. Pigeons hang mid-flap. The counter drops to 10.
You run.
> SCENE: You sprint into the road, scarf streaming. The counter falls on each word.
Nine. Eight. Seven.
> SCENE: You lift the frozen kid off the crossing and set them down on the pavement. The ball stays in the road.
Six. Five. Four.
> SCENE: You step back onto the kerb, breathing hard.
Three. Two. One.
> SCENE: Colour returns. The bus brakes, horn blaring, through the crossing, and flattens the ball. The kid blinks at the pavement.
The bus screams through an empty crossing. The ball doesn't make it. The kid does.
> SCENE: Wide, side-on. The bus at rest past the crossing. A paper tape measure unrolls from its bumper back up the road.
> TAG: 23 M
A car at 48 kilometres an hour needs about 23 metres to stop (c14). And this is a bus.
> SCENE: Close on the kerb: the kid's cap. Then the kid, picking it up.
Nobody could have stopped that bus. You didn't need to. You just needed ten seconds.
> SCENE: The kid's mother runs over and hugs the kid, then turns to thank the shaken bus driver. You stand at the edge of the frame.
The kid's mother runs over, shaking. She thanks the bus driver. Nobody thanks you. Nobody knows to.
> SCENE: You walk away down the street, hands in pockets. Your hands tremble.
You walk home the long way. Your hands won't stop shaking.

## [03:30] The catch
> SCENE: Night. Close on your hands, still trembling. Then your face in the dark. Then you at the window, looking down at the street.
You saved a life with ten seconds. That feels free.
It is not free.
Your body keeps ageing through every pause. Ten seconds a day that nobody else lives.
And now you know what ten seconds can do. So you can't stop watching.
> SCENE: Morning. Kitchen. Your elbow knocks the mug. Your hand twitches towards a snap.
Then one morning you're tired. The mug tips. Coffee, or keep your seconds just in case?
> SCENE: You snap. Grey. You catch the mug. Colour. You sip, pleased with yourself.
Just this once. Coffee.
> SCENE: That afternoon. Stairs at a metro station. An old man misses a step. You snap. Nothing happens: the counter reads 0.
That afternoon, an old man misses a step on the station stairs. You snap your fingers. Nothing.
> SCENE: The old man falls the last three steps. People rush to him. He sits up, rubbing his arm.
He's all right. Bruised. You're not.
> SCENE: Night. You in bed, eyes open, the counter on the wall reading 10.
That night, you lie awake doing the maths of every second you've ever wasted.
> TAG: MONTH 6
> SCENE: Montage, two seconds each, the counter top right reading 10: you at the crossing, watching. You on a bus, watching. You on the stairs, watching.
You never spend them on coffee again. You keep them in your pocket. Every single day.
> SCENE: Bedroom, midnight. The counter still reads 10. You lie awake, then snap. Grey. The city outside the window hangs still.
Some days nothing happens. So at midnight you spend them on nothing at all.
Ten seconds of a city holding its breath, just for you.
> SCENE: Across the street, in a frozen grey building, a curtain in one window swings shut. The counter flips to 0. Colour. You stare at the window.
Except, across the street, a curtain moves. In a window that should be frozen.

## [04:30] The long run
> SCENE: You at your window, night after night, watching the building across the street. The curtain never moves.
You watch that window for a week. The curtain never moves again. So, slowly, you get on with your life again.
> SCENE: A daydream cloud over your head: you in front of a bank vault, the counter at 10.
People always ask what you'd do with a superpower. Rob a bank?
Ten seconds gets you to the counter. It doesn't open a vault.
> SCENE: The cloud pops. Street cricket in a lane. A ball skied high towards the boundary wall. You snap. Grey. You walk under the frozen ball, hands cupped. Colour. It drops into your hands. The lane erupts.
Catch the six that wins your lane's cricket match? That, you do. Just the once.
It turns out ten seconds is too small for greed. It's exactly the right size for an accident.
> SCENE: A cyclist wobbles towards a bottle in the road. You snap, nudge the bottle into the gutter and step back. Colour. The cyclist rides on.
> SCENE: Quick cuts, two seconds each: a pan handle you turn inward; a toddler you lift back from the top stair; a gate you latch.
A bottle under a bike wheel. A pan about to tip. A toddler at the top of the stairs.
You become the reason small disasters don't happen.
> SCENE: You at a café table with your best friend. You open your mouth. Then close it.
Do you tell your best friend? You open your mouth. Then you close it.
Who would believe you? And what would they ask you to pause for?
> TAG: YEAR 40
> SCENE: The same street through the seasons: leaves, rain, snow, sun. You age a little in each. In one frozen winter frame, far back, the figure in the long dark coat stands among the grey crowd, facing you.
> SCENE: You, older, grey in the tuft, at a corner. Your eyes flick to a wobbling ladder, a loose dog, a driver looking at a phone.
Forty years in, you read a street like a weather map. A wobbling ladder. A loose dog. A driver looking at a phone.
You see the accident before it happens. Most days, you don't even need the pause.
> SCENE: Two paper clocks side by side. Yours gives a small extra tick every day.
Your body lives every one of those pauses. Over eighty years, they add up to about three days and nine hours.
> SCENE: A mirror. You squint at your reflection. Beside it, not on your face, a tag.
> TAG: 3 DAYS 9 HOURS
By eighty, that's how much older you'll be than everyone you grew up with. You'd barely notice.
> SCENE: Pull back from the mirror to the window, and the street below full of strangers.
The bigger cost is the watching. A whole life spent ten seconds at a time, on strangers who'll never know your name.

## [06:15] Back to you
> SCENE: The crossing from day twelve, ordinary and busy. A car approaches; the split-flap counter shows a sliver of a second.
Here's the real part. Nobody gets ten frozen seconds. A real driver gets under one, before the brakes even touch (c14).
> SCENE: You, weighing a coffee mug in one hand and the counter in the other.
So what would you do with your ten seconds? Spend them every morning, or save them for a day that might never come?
Tell us below. The best what-ifs become videos.
> SCENE: The street, evening. You snap. The crowd freezes, grey. You turn to walk home.
And then you notice something.
> SCENE: In the frozen crowd, the figure in the long dark coat, with the crimson head, is walking.
Someone else is still moving.
> SCENE: The stranger stops. Turns. Looks straight at you.
And they're looking right at you.
> BUBBLE: The stranger: "Nice catch. Day twelve."
> SCENE: Black for one second. Then a quiet end card: you, small, in the left two-thirds; the right third empty for end screens.

## Narration notes (for G3; not spoken)
- Each line is one take. Feed the voice a spelled-out text and keep the digits in this script for the claims check:
  "0.45" → "nought point four five"; "10 to 14 metres" → "ten to fourteen metres"; "23 metres ... 48 kilometres an hour" → "twenty-three metres ... forty-eight kilometres an
  hour";
- "Chronostasis": respell for the voice as "krono-STAY-sis".
- The countdown ("Nine. Eight. Seven." to "Three. Two. One.") is one take with a break between numbers, sliced to
  the counter, and kept out of the 1.1 speed-up so each number lasts about a second. Steady, a little breathless.
- One- and two-word lines ("Pause.", "You run.", "Vanish.", "Not today.", "Just this once. Coffee.") are generated inside a carrier phrase and
  trimmed. "You hit pause." and "It is not free.": a beat before, quiet. The last three lines: slower, hushed.
