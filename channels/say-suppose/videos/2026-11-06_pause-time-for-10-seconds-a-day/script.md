# Script: What If You Could Pause Time for 10 Seconds a Day?

format: story
status: draft for G2
claims: claims.md

## [00:00] The change
> SCENE: Bedroom, morning. The alarm clock rings. You slap it, and the room drains to grey. Dust hangs in the light.
You wake up and discover you can pause time.
The alarm is frozen mid-ring. The dust hangs in the air. Your cat is stuck halfway off the bed.
So, what if you could pause time for ten seconds a day?
> SCENE: Colour floods back. The cat lands. The alarm finishes its ring.

## [00:20] The rule
> TAG: 10 SECONDS
Ten seconds. Once a day. Then the world starts again, and nobody knows.
Everyone and everything stops. Except you.
Now, a confession before we start. Real physics would wreck this power in a second.
If time really stopped, the air around you would stop too, and you would be trapped in it like a fly in glass (c01).
And light would stop reaching your eyes, so you would stand there blind (c02).
So we cheat on exactly two things. Air moves for you, and you can see. Everything else in this video is real.
> SCENE: You test it. Pause. You wave your hand through the frozen air. It parts like water.
Even the atomic clocks freeze with everyone else. They define a second as 9,192,631,770 ticks (c03), and they miss every one of yours.

## [00:50] The first hours
> SCENE: Kitchen. You knock your coffee mug off the counter.
At first, you use it for dumb things.
> SCENE: The mug tips over the edge.
A falling mug hits the floor in about 0.45 seconds (c04). Nobody catches that. Nobody human.
Pause.
> SCENE: Grey. The mug hangs in the air, coffee curling out of it like a frozen ribbon.
You stroll over. You have time. People walk about 1.4 metres a second, so ten seconds gets you around 14 metres (c05).
> SCENE: You pluck the mug out of the air and turn it upright. A smirk.
You catch your falling coffee.
> SCENE: Colour returns. You sip.
So what else fits into ten seconds? Take a guess.
The fastest 100 metres ever run took 9.58 seconds (c06). So, in theory, a sprint.
You are not that fast. You could type about 9 words, at the average speed (c07). Your heart, if you were calm, would beat 10 to 17 times (c08).
> SCENE: Hallway at work. Your boss steps out of an office, waving.
> BUBBLE: Boss: "Got a minu—"
Or you could dodge your boss in the hallway.
> SCENE: Grey. The boss frozen mid-wave. You tiptoe past, under his arm.
Ten seconds is long enough to vanish.
> SCENE: Colour. The boss blinks at the empty hallway.
Here is the strange part. You have felt a tiny pause before.
Ever glanced at a clock and the second hand seemed to stick for a moment?
That is real. Scientists call it chronostasis (c09).
It happens every time you move your eyes. You only notice when there is a clock to catch it (c10).
Your brain has been editing time for you all your life. You just got the remote.

## [02:30] The first year
> SCENE: Montage. Day after day, the world goes grey for a moment: a dropped pen caught, a closing lift door held, a parking spot taken.
Ten seconds a day comes to about an hour a year.
An hour nobody else gets.
You get good at it. You learn the rhythm of a pause. Three seconds to look. Seven to act.
You learn which ten seconds matter.
> TAG: DAY 12
Then, on day twelve, you are walking home.
> SCENE: Street, afternoon. Crowds, pigeons, a crossing. A bus is coming down the road.
A bus runs a red light.
> SCENE: A small kid in a blue cap chases a ball onto the crossing.
There is a kid in the road.
Roads are the most dangerous thing in this kid's life.
Crashes kill about 1.16 million people a year (c11).
For anyone aged 5 to 29, they are the leading cause of death (c12).
And a driver cannot stop on a thought. Road designers allow 1.5 seconds just to notice the danger, and 2.5 seconds before the brakes even bite (c13).
Even a car at 48 kilometres an hour needs about 23 metres to stop (c14). This is a bus.
So here is your choice. Do you shout? Do you run?
Neither works. Not in time.
You hit pause.
> SCENE: The world drains to grey from you outward. The bus stops, a metre from the kid. Pigeons hang mid-flap.
> TAG: 10
You run.
> SCENE: You sprint into the road, scarf streaming. The counter falls.
Nine. Eight. Seven.
> SCENE: You lift the frozen kid off the crossing and set them on the pavement, ball and all.
Six. Five. Four.
> SCENE: You step back onto the kerb, breathing hard.

## [03:30] The catch
Three.
> SCENE: Colour returns. The bus brakes, horn blaring, through an empty crossing. The kid blinks at the pavement.
The bus screams through an empty crossing. The kid has no idea why they are suddenly on the kerb.
You saved a life with ten seconds. That feels free.
It is not free.
Every pause, you live ten seconds that nobody else does. Your body ages through them.
Time running at different speeds for different people sounds made up. It is not.
Astronauts on the space station come home 0.007 seconds younger than if they had stayed on the ground (c15).
Your own head ages faster than your feet. Over 79 years, it adds up to about 90 billionths of a second (c16).
Tiny. And yet the world notices.
GPS satellites run fast by about 38 microseconds a day. Ignore that, and your map drifts by about 10 kilometres a day (c17).
So, would your ten seconds matter?
> SCENE: You, at a mirror, squinting at your face.
Eighty years of pauses add up to about three and a half days. You would be three and a half days older than your twin.
No satellite would notice. No doctor would.
The cost is not your age.

## [04:30] The long run
> SCENE: A calendar flipping. You at the same crossing, again and again, watching.
The cost is that you can never stop watching.
Once you know ten seconds can save a kid, every second you do not use feels like a choice.
Do you save your pause for a crisis that might never come? Or spend it on coffee?
> SCENE: You hold a pause in reserve, hand hovering, all day. Nothing happens.
You start keeping it until evening. Just in case.
Some days nothing happens, and you use it at midnight to finish a sentence before anyone can interrupt.
Some days, you are glad you waited.
> SCENE: A cyclist wobbles; you pause, nudge a stray bottle off the road, step back.
> SCENE: Daydream bubbles: you in front of a bank vault; you at an exam desk.
People always ask what you would do with a superpower. Rob a bank?
In ten seconds you cover about 14 metres (c05). That will not get you past the queue, let alone the vault.
Cheat in an exam? An adult reads about 238 words a minute (c18). Ten seconds buys you about 40 words. Maybe one answer.
It turns out ten seconds is too small for greed. It is exactly the right size for an accident.
Over a lifetime, that is about three and a half days of borrowed time.
Spent ten seconds at a time on strangers who will never know your name.
And that is when you start to wonder.
If one person got this power, why would it be only one?

## [06:15] Back to you
> SCENE: The street again. You hit pause. The crowd freezes, grey. You turn to walk away.
And then you notice something.
> SCENE: In the frozen crowd, one figure in a long dark coat, crimson head, is walking.
Someone else is still moving.
> SCENE: The stranger stops. Turns. Looks straight at you.
And they are looking right at you.
> SCENE: Blackout.
Ten seconds will not make you rich, or powerful. But it is just enough to change one thing. And see what follows.
What would you do with your ten seconds? Tell us in the comments. The best what-ifs become videos.
