# Research: AI rented your brain while you slept (G2)

Read on 2026-11-13, in this session: a research agent fetched every page (curl and WebFetch) and returned verbatim
quotes; the session read each quote against the claim it supports and narrowed the wording where the quote is narrower.
The spoken claims, their quotes and their status are in `claims.md` (c01-c25); this file is the reading behind them and
why the film is set where it is.

## Why El Paso, a spring of Thursdays

The idea needs a place where renting your body by the hour is already an ordinary errand. The US is one of the few
countries that let companies pay plasma donors (ProPublica 2019, c01; NPR 2021: "one of five countries"), federal rules
let a donor give twice in any seven days with at least two days between (21 CFR 640.65, c02), and about two thirds of
the world's plasma comes from the US (NPR Planet Money 2021, c03; ProPublica 2022 gives about 60%, Annals of Blood 74% of
source plasma in 2016, so "about two thirds" and never "70%"). In 2019, 43 of the 805 US plasma centres sat along the
southern border (c04), and people crossed from Ciudad Juárez to sell plasma in El Paso twice a week, where a typical
worker across the river made about nine dollars a day (c05, c22); up to a tenth of US plasma came from Mexicans on
visitor visas until a 2021 ban, which a judge lifted by injunction in 2022 (c23; its status since was not checked, so the
film says nothing about it). So the film's first picture, a clinic chair at dawn and a legal cap on how often your body
can be sold, is real, and the band is the same errand without the cap.

Weighed: Nairobi, where Kenyan workers labelling data to train ChatGPT for Sama were paid between about $1.32 and $2 an
hour (TIME 2023, c09). It is the right place for the falling-price beat and the film uses it there, but it gives one row
and no picture of a body sold by the clock. And no place at all: it loses the chair, the bridge and the floor price.

## The brain and sleep

- The brain is about 2% of body weight and accounts for about 20% of the body's oxygen and calories (Raichle and
  Gusnard, PNAS 2002); NIST puts its power at about 20 watts (c10). Neither says "at rest", so the film doesn't.
- About a third of our time is spent asleep (NINDS, c11).
- In live mice, natural sleep (or anaesthesia) came with a 60% increase in the space between brain cells and faster
  clearing of waste (Xie et al., Science 2013, c12). The abstract doesn't use "glymphatic"; the film says "in mice".
- Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924): two people forgot less nonsense syllables after sleep than after the same hours awake
  (Rasch and Born, Physiol Rev 2013, c13); in rats, the day's firing patterns replay during sleep, 10 to 20 times faster
  (same review, c14).
- Randy Gardner, 17, stayed awake 11 days and 25 minutes in 1964 for a science fair; his cognitive and sensory
  abilities began to be affected (BBC 2018, c15). Speech problems: no source, dropped.
- More than a third of American adults don't get at least seven hours on a regular basis (CDC 2016, c24).
- One night without sleep cut hippocampal activity while forming new memories and worsened later recall (Yoo et al.,
  Nature Neuroscience 2007, c25). The well-known "40%" is from an unpublished pilot in Walker's lab, not this paper.
- Dolphins sleep one half of the brain at a time (Mascetti 2016, c20).
- Otto Loewi's prize-winning frog-heart experiment came to him in a dream, in 1920, published 1921; Nobel 1936, shared
  with Dale (McCoy and Tan 2014, c21).

## Minds rented already

- reCAPTCHA had people type the words computers couldn't read, digitising the New York Times archive and Google Books;
  the 2008 Science paper says more than 100 million CAPTCHAs a day (NYT 2011's later estimate: 200 million) (c16).
- Amazon Mechanical Turk, launched in 2005, paid a few cents a task and was named after an 18th-century chess "robot"
  that hid a human chess master; it closed on 30 September 2026 (CNBC, c17), so the film uses the past tense.
- In 2020 over a million volunteers' computers in Folding@home made what its scientists called the first exascale
  computer, about 1.01 exaflops (Zimmerman et al., Nature Chemistry 2021, c18). "First" is the authors' claim; the film
  says "its scientists said" and never "exascale".
- Cortical Labs (Kagan et al., Neuron 2022): about 800,000 mouse brain cells on a chip showed "apparent learning" at
  Pong within five minutes (c19). The film says "began to get better", and "the old bat-and-ball video game".
- Before the 2021 Super Bowl, Molson Coors ran a "dream incubation" stunt (Harvard Petrie-Flom 2022, c07); about 40
  sleep and dream scientists, led by Stickgold, Zadra and Haar, signed an open letter against advertising in dreams
  (UCI News 2021, c08). The ad never aired during the game (Gizmodo 2021), so the film never says it reached a Super
  Bowl audience.
