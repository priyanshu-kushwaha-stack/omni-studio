## 4. Story

### Does the Story Work?
* **What works (KEEP):**
  1. **The Brother relationship & "Crumbs" ritual:** The younger brother (hanging upside down from the top bunk, testing two bands under his hat, keeping the crumb notebook, putting `"YOU"` / `"KEYS"` / `"DOOR"` stickers on everything, and saying *"Fine. I'll fail something. You can come to the retake"* after you forget his graduation) gives an abstract sci-fi concept genuine human warmth and stakes.
  2. **The Economic Escalation (`4:37–5:35` & `8:16–8:56`):** Bus naps for $4, the clinic closing at noon because the nurse wears a band, the boss taking a 20% pillow cut on a workplace nap room, the grandmother next door becoming the highest earner on the block, coffee shops closing while the city goes to bed at 7 PM, the border sign (*"NO SLEEPING ON A VISITOR VISA"*), and the 1,000-cot warehouse when the rate crashes from $90 to $12—this world-building escalation is smart, funny, and darkly believable.
  3. **The Hive-Mind Climax Concept:** Discovering that *"One brain, one band, one jar"* was a lie—that the neighbor humming your shower tune, the shared face drawings, and the crumbs are cross-talk from millions of brains networked into one supercomputer—is a terrific payoff.

* **What does NOT work (FIX):**
  1. **The "Leaflet" structure kills Act 1 (`1:21–2:38`):** Having characters sit at a kitchen table for 77 seconds reading a pamphlet aloud is anti-cinema. Worse, the leaflet states rules (*"One brain, one band, one jar"*, *"crumbs"*, *"20 watts"*) before we have seen any mystery in action. Show the weird crumbs *first* (`2:39–3:40`), let the audience wonder why he's humming the neighbor's tune and folding fitted sheets, and let the brother read the fine print in 15 seconds to explain what's happening.
  2. **Four separate *"It's True"* Wikipedia tangents stall the plot:**
     * `2:11–2:22`: 2022 DishBrain Pong cells
     * `4:15–4:29`: 1921 Otto Loewi frog-heart dream
     * `6:35–7:00`: Mouse glymphatic trash-washing + rat maze replay
     * `8:21–8:40`: US-Mexico plasma supply statistics
     * `8:57–9:10`: 18th-century Mechanical Turk chess robot
     * `10:53–11:18`: 2020 Folding@home distributed computers
     That is **six real-world trivia sidebars totaling ~2 minutes 15 seconds**, each pulling us out of the characters' world onto a white diagram card. Keep brief, 1-sentence nods to **two** at most (glymphatic trash/memory filing during the memory-loss montage, and distributed computing during the hive-mind reveal) and cut the rest.
  3. **Muddled "Three Problems" signposting:**
     * `"Problem 1"` (`4:30.8`) is *"Ninety dollars for eight hours. So what's twelve worth?"*—which leads into both the citywide sleep boom (`4:37–5:35`) AND his 12-hour memory loss (`5:58–7:15`).
     * `"Problem 2"` (`7:15.9`) is *"Whatever you lose, you lose alone"*—which is just a continuation of the exact same memory loss we already watched for 72 seconds (`6:03–7:15`)!
     * Make the three escalations clean and distinct: **Problem 1: Greed / The Sleep Rush** (everyone sleeps for money; the city shuts down at 7 PM). **Problem 2: The Cost** (pushing to 12 hours erases your waking memories—keys, PIN, front door, your brother's graduation). **Problem 3: The Crash** (oversupply drops the rate from $90 to $12; only rested brains get hired).
  4. **Plot hole at `9:33–9:59` (Why does the Broker offer YOU triple pay?):** At `9:10–9:30`, the film establishes that sleep-deprived brains are worthless (*"Rested ones only"*) and your brother is turned away at the warehouse door. Yet 10 seconds later (`9:40`), the Broker offers *you*—the guy who hasn't had real sleep in six weeks and can't remember his own front door—a **triple-pay year-long contract**! Why? **Fix:** Add one line explaining *why* the Broker wants you: because unlike casual sleepers who wake after 8 hours, you've trained yourself to stay under for 12 uninterrupted hours—making your brain a prized "anchor node" for the network's final assembly.
  5. **Contradiction between the Climax ("never needs sleepers") and the Epilogue ("this one's for daytime"):** At `11:34.8`, the big reveal is that the sleepers are designing *"the thing that will never need sleepers."* Why, then, does the Broker show up on Thursday (`12:57.1`) selling a **daytime band for humans** (*"This one's for daytime. Everyone else already said yes"*)? If the machine never needs sleepers, why does the company now need daytime wearers? **Fix:** Connect the two! Either:
     * **(Option A — The Machine Wears Us):** The blueprint the sleepers built *is* the daytime band—a machine that doesn't need you to sleep anymore because it runs on your waking brain while your body goes on autopilot. *"The sleepers weren't designing a computer to replace human brains. They were designing the upgrade that didn't have to wait for bedtime."*
     * **(Option B — Unfinished Build):** Because you and others refused the final contract—or because sleeping brains couldn't finish the last 10%—the company rolled out the daytime band to harvest waking minds. Option A is much tighter and makes the `11:24` blueprint and `13:06` daytime-zombie street shot one unified twist!

## 7. Pacing and Length

### Is 13:22 Right?
**No.** For this story, **13:22 is ~3.5 minutes too long.** The story has enough narrative fuel for a killer **9:35–9:50** video. Everything that makes the film feel slow comes from three sources:
1. **Exposition duplicates** (the Leaflet repeating the opening rules; four separate real-world science/history lectures on white cards).
2. **Dead-air gaps between lines** (there are **14 separate pauses of 3 to 10 seconds** with static or barely moving visuals, totaling nearly **60 seconds** of dead air).
3. **Held shots and repeated transition cards** (blank white pages held for 2 seconds before diagrams start; 18 seconds of holding the leaflet at the kitchen table; 17 seconds in the bunk bed at noon).
