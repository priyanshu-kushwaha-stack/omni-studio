# Packaging: AI rented your brain while you slept

status: G1 draft, round 1 (2026-11-13). Written by Claude Fable 5.1 (`claude -p`)
topic: 2026-11-12 AI-and-the-future slate, f-ai-rents-your-sleeping-brain, Fable's pitch, scored 78/100 blind (1st of 24)

## Premise

A thin dark band with one teal light sits across your forehead. While you sleep, a company runs its work on your brain, and every morning the band pays you by the hour. You feel nothing. You remember nothing. Night one pays ninety dollars, the best wage of your life, for lying down.

**The catch.** The hours were never spare. Sleep is when your brain takes out the trash and files your day, and a rented night does neither: your keys go, your PIN goes, the name of your street goes, and a day you lived and photographed is never filed at all. Every night also leaves a crumb that isn't yours, a taste, a tune, a word, a face. And the price is a market: as the whole border signs up the pay per hour falls, the best-rested brains fetch the most, and the people who need the money most earn the least. The crumbs are the one thing the band lets you keep, and they are the only way to find out what the job is.

**Why this setting.** El Paso, Texas, in dollars, on the border with Mexico, over about six weeks of one spring. The engine of this idea is that renting out your body by the hour is already an ordinary errand, and no place in the ledger has that built into the furniture the way this border does. The US lets companies pay for blood plasma, the law caps a donor at twice in seven days (c02), about two thirds of the world's plasma comes from this one country (c03), and people cross the bridge from the Mexican side twice a week to sell it in El Paso because a day's work over there pays about nine dollars (c22, c05). So the film's first picture is real: a clinic chair at dawn, a clock, a legal limit on how often your body can be sold. The band is the same errand without the limit, and the law's "twice a week" is the film's first mirror: the state caps your blood; nobody caps your nights. The queue is also the market in miniature: when the price falls, the people already crossing a bridge to sell themselves are the ones who cross it to sleep in a warehouse. I weighed two alternatives. Nairobi, Kenya, where workers labelling data to train ChatGPT were paid under two dollars an hour (c09): the right place for the falling-price beat, and the film uses that fact there, but the ledger gives it one row and no picture of a body sold by the clock, and the idea's first act needs that chair. And no place at all, a generic bedroom anywhere: it loses the chair, the bridge and the floor price, and leaves the market half of the film with nothing real under it. El Paso wins on seven rows and one chair. Plain words still rule: the narration says "the clinic", "the pale part of your blood", "the bridge", "across the river" and "the other side"; the city is named once, in the narrator's second line, and no story detail needs a local word or name to follow.

**Invented:** you, your brother, the man in the grey suit, the nurse, your boss, the grandmother next door, the warehouse clerk, the band, every rate it pays and every amount in the jar, the leaflet, the premium contract, the crumb notebook, the warehouses of cots, the face everyone dreams, the machine the crumbs draw. **Real:** paid plasma capped at twice in seven days with two days between (c02); about two thirds of the world's plasma from the US (c03); Mexican citizens crossing into El Paso to sell plasma, some twice a week, where a typical worker across the river makes about nine dollars a day (c05, c22); the brain at about two per cent of body weight using about twenty per cent of its energy, about twenty watts (c10); about eight hundred thousand brain cells in a dish that began to get better at Pong within five minutes (c19); reCAPTCHA's squiggly words digitising old newspapers and Google Books, more than a hundred million typed a day (c16); Otto Loewi's prize-winning experiment coming to him in a dream, done on a frog heart (c21); a third of life spent asleep (c11); in mice, the space between brain cells about sixty per cent wider in sleep and waste washed out faster (c12); the 1924 test on two people who forgot less after sleeping (c13) and, in rats, the day's activity replayed in sleep faster than real time (c14); Randy Gardner's eleven days awake in 1964 for a science fair, his thinking suffering by the end (c15); more than a third of American adults short of sleep (c24); Kenyan data labellers paid less than two dollars an hour (c09); Mechanical Turk, launched in 2005, paying a few cents a task, named after an eighteenth-century chess robot with a human hidden inside (c17); Coors's 2021 dream-incubation stunt and the open letter from about forty sleep scientists led by Stickgold, Zadra and Haar (c07, c08); over a million volunteers' idle computers forming what Folding@home's scientists called the first exascale computer in 2020 (c18).

## Cast

- **You.** Mustard head, teal scarf. Thursdays at dawn you sit in the clinic chair and watch the clock, and you count every waking hour as money you did or didn't earn. You never speak; at most a bubble.
- **Your brother.** Peach head, a cap, the top bunk. Final year of school; his graduation is on a Saturday at the film's 60%. Thinks the band is a scam until the jar fills, then wears one under his cap. Keeps a notebook of crumbs. Every morning, palm out: "What'd you get?" The person to lose: not him, the memory of him.
- **The man in the grey suit.** The broker. Grey head, a shoebox of bands at the clinic door. "Sleep tonight. Get paid." Comes back with the contract, and at the end with a new box.
- **The nurse.** Sage head, a paper cap. "Thursday. Not before." By 2:40 there's a band under the cap.
- **Your boss.** Tie. Opens a nap room at work with a till. "Twenty per cent. Pillows are mine."
- **The grandmother next door.** Lilac head. Out-earns the street because she sleeps eleven hours. "Finally."
- **The warehouse clerk.** Clipboard. "Rested ones only."
- **The stranger.** Hidden once, in the wide shot of the street gone to bed at seven (3:00), walking under a lamp. Crimson head, long coat. No line.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: El Paso, dawn, the clinic chair where you sell the pale part of your blood twice a week, the most the law allows, counting every hour as money. A band on your forehead turns your nights into the best wage of your life.
> DESIRE: To get out of the chair for good and clear what the family owes, by doing nothing.
> CONFLICT: The hours you sell were never spare: they were the shift your brain worked for you, and now your days go unfiled while the price of a night falls for everyone.
> CHANGE: You stop counting hours as money and start asking what the hours were for.
> RESULT: The band in the drawer. You sleep for free, wake knowing your street and yesterday's faces, and bring back one dream that is only yours.
> UNDER: What am I actually selling when I sell my time?
> DARLING: "Sleep was never nothing. It was the one shift you always worked for yourself."
> DARLING: "You can't sleep on it. Sleeping is the job."
> DARLING: "The biggest idea people ever had together, and nobody was awake for it."

**How it differs from the nearest video.** Kurzgesagt-style sleep explainers tell you why sleep matters with nothing at stake; here every fact costs you something you can name, keys, a PIN, your brother's graduation. Severance splits a working day into a self who works and a self who doesn't, and its horror is the office; here the night itself is rented for cash, the catch is that the night was never idle, and the turn is that the rented minds were never separate: one shared mind, paid to design the thing that won't need it, and nobody awake for the biggest idea people ever had together. No "paid to sleep" or "AI uses your brain" what-if in the last ninety days has the plasma-border floor under it or the collective-mind turn.
