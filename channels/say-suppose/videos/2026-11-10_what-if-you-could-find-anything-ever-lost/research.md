# Research: find anything ever lost

Fetched and read on 2026-11-11 (G2). Every spoken claim, its exact quote and its source are in `claims.md`; each quote
was checked against the saved page text in `sources/` (one file per page, URL and fetch date on the first line). This
file is the reading behind them, and what was looked at and not used.

## What the sources say

- **The 1878 Act (c01).** The Indian Treasure-trove Act, 1878 (Act No. 6 of 1878, 13 February 1878), India Code
  text. "Treasure" is "anything of any value hidden in the soil, or in anything affixed thereto" (s. 3). Section 4:
  whenever treasure "exceeding in amount or value ten rupees" is found, the finder must, as soon as practicable, give
  the Collector notice in writing (what it is and roughly what it is worth, where, and when it was found) and either
  deposit it in the nearest Government treasury or give the Collector security to produce it. Section 20: a finder
  who does not give the notice, or make the deposit or give the security, loses his share to Government and can be
  imprisoned for up to one year, or fined, or both. Useful for the restart beat: under s. 8, if the treasure was hidden
  within one hundred years and someone claims it, the Collector adjourns the case so the claimant can sue, so the dead
  owner's family in the lane has a real way to claim it; when the owner of the place claims and the finder doesn't
  dispute it, the finder gets three-fourths (s. 12). Maharashtra (where Pune is) amended only sections 1 and 22, so
  sections 4 and 20 apply there as printed. India Code itself refused the fetcher (403); the text was read from the
  Wayback Machine's copy of the same India Code PDF (12 July 2025). The earbud in a drain grate is not "treasure" under
  s. 3 (not hidden in the soil); the bungalow coins buried in the compound are.
- **The men who sift the drains (c02).** Three reports, three cities, three names. AFP (Gulf News, 22 October 2014),
  Kolkata's Bowbazar: "newaras", gold dust scavengers, sift the sludge in the alleys and drains for particles washed
  out of about 2,000 jewellery workshops; Mohammad Iqbal, 40, works "early one morning" over pans of black muck, and
  says it was his grandfather's and father's work and is now his sons'. Christian Science Monitor (26 March 2010),
  Mumbai's Zaveri Bazaar: street sweepers pan for gold "amid the sewage pipes in front of goldsmiths' homes"; one,
  Samir Sheikh, says the goldsmiths' shower water carries the dust into the drain and "We then collect the mud in the
  gutter and look for the gold." ETV Bharat (5 November 2026), Gaya, Bihar: the "Nyari or Dhul Dhoya community" (also
  "Nyaria") buy a year's workshop dust ("Nyara") from jewellers and recover the gold, with water and mercury. So the
  word nyariya is real, but in the source it names the people who buy and wash workshop dust, not the drain-sifters;
  the drain-sifters are "newaras" in Kolkata and "street sweepers" in Mumbai. The last image (a man sifting the grate
  at dawn) is supported by AFP's early-morning and after-dawn scenes; the script should not call him a nyariya on
  screen or in words unless it says "dust-washer" loosely, and the "forty years" in the beat outline is not in any
  source (Iqbal is 40 years old; that is his age, not his years at it). Keep it as story.
- **The Newport landfill (c03).** The High Court judgment, Howells v Newport City Council [2025] EWHC 22 (Ch), 9
  January 2025 (HHJ Keyser KC): in August 2013 a hard drive with the private key to his Bitcoin went "in error" to
  Docksway Landfill Site, "owned and operated by the defendant, Newport City Council". On his pleaded facts (assumed,
  not found, for the application): he mined 8,000 Bitcoin in early 2009; on 4 August 2013 he put the wrong drive in a
  bin bag, and the next morning his partner took the bags to the site; he first met the council about it on 25
  November 2013 and then made repeated requests for access. The landfill holds about 350,000 tonnes, plus 50,000 a
  year; a 2021 report for him puts the drive "probably" in about 2,000 square metres and 10,000-15,000 tonnes of waste.
  Digging is a criminal offence without the council's authority, and the council would need a new environmental
  permit. Judgment for the council; claim dismissed. BBC News (7 August 2025): he has given up on retrieving it, owes
  the council just under £117,000 in costs, his appeal was rejected, the council plans to close the site in 2025-26,
  and he now means to launch a token (Ceiniog Coin) instead. So the beat's "is still asking" is out of date: he asked
  from 2013 to 2025 and then stopped.
- **Wrecks (c04).** UNESCO's own Underwater Cultural Heritage page, "Wrecks": "An estimated 3 million shipwrecks are
  spread across ocean floors around the planet." The live URL now redirects to UNESCO's general culture page, so the
  text was read from the Wayback Machine's 22 March 2017 copy (the 2025 copy is a bot-check page). UNESCO's wording here
  is "an estimated 3 million", not "over 3 million" (that phrase is in the 2007 Convention booklet, which refused the
  fetcher; not read).
- **Lost bitcoin (c05, optional).** Cointelegraph (6 February 2022), quoting Kim Grauer, Chainalysis's director of
  research: an estimated 3.7 million BTC lost, "close to 20%" of circulating supply; she called the estimate "a bit
  old". An estimate, via a news report, not Chainalysis's own page.
- **Household gold (c06, optional).** World Gold Council, India gold market series (12 July 2023): Indian households
  hold "an estimated tonnage of up to 25,000", footnoted to Metals Focus. No beat uses it now.

## Looked at, not used

- How much time people spend looking for lost things (c07): every figure found is a commercial poll (Pixie's 2016
  survey of 1,700 US adults, "2.5 days" a year; a Shane Co. poll publicised by Tile). Self-reported, by companies that
  sell trackers. Skipped.
- Hindustan Times (17 June 2018) on Delhi's Karol Bagh and Chandni Chowk drain-sifters: only the PressReader page was
  reachable and it returned no article text, so nothing from it is used.
- The 110,000+ tonnes in the beat outline: not found. The judgment's figures (about 350,000 tonnes in the landfill;
  the drive within 10,000-15,000 tonnes) are the sourced ones if the cross-section needs a number. The "eleven
  metres" of the cross-section is the story's.
- UNESCO's "over 3 million undiscovered shipwrecks" (2007 Convention booklet on unesdoc.unesco.org): 403 to the
  fetcher, so the claim uses the Wrecks page's wording instead.
- The Act's state amendments (Bihar, Madras, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh) are listed in the India Code footnote but not
  printed there; a bare-act site reporting one of them refused the fetcher. Not needed for a film set in Maharashtra.
