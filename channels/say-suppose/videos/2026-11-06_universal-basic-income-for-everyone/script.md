# Script: What If Everyone Got Free Money Every Month?

status: draft for G2

## [00:00] The change
> SCENE: Your bedroom, early morning. Your phone buzzes on the bedside table. You squint at it.
Your phone buzzes. Money has landed in your account.
> SCENE: Out of the window, phones light up in every flat across the street.
So has everyone else's. The same amount, for every adult in the country.
It will land again next month. And the month after. Forever.
What would happen if everyone got free money every month?

## [00:20] The rule
> TAG: NO STRINGS
There are no forms. No conditions. Rich or poor, working or not, you get it.
It is enough for food and rent. It is not enough to be comfortable.
You do not have to do anything to keep it.
So here is the first question. Tomorrow is Monday. Do you go to work?
> SCENE: You, at the door, coat half on, hesitating.

## [00:50] The first months
> SCENE: You at your desk, staring at the boss's office. A daydream bubble: you walking out, coin overhead.
You think about quitting. Everyone thinks about quitting.
But people have been in your shoes. Lots of them.
> SCENE: A map pins Alaska. A cheque flutters down to every house.
Since 1982, every resident of Alaska has been paid a yearly dividend, about $2,000 a person in recent years (c01). In 2025 it was $1,000 (c02).
Did Alaskans quit? No. Researchers found no effect on employment, and part-time work rose 17% (c03).
Some people worked a little less. Others started working at all.
> SCENE: The map swings to Kenya. A village, mobile phones buzzing in every hand.
In Kenya, every adult in 44 villages gets $0.75 a day, paid monthly, for 12 years (c04).
That is real money there. So did they stop working?
They did not. Work did not change overall. People moved from wage jobs to working for themselves (c05).
> SCENE: Your neighbour wheels a sewing machine home. Another opens a tea stall.
In those villages, the number of businesses rose 14%, and their revenues 41% (c06).
> SCENE: Finland. Snow. A queue outside a job centre.
Finland tried it on people without jobs: 2,000 of them got €560 a month (c07).
In the first year, they worked no more than anyone else. In the second year, about six days more, and the researchers say even that cannot be put down to the money alone (c08).
What changed most was how they felt. They reported being more satisfied with their lives, and less stressed, depressed and lonely (c09).
> SCENE: You, back at your desk. You do not quit. But you finally ask for a raise.
So you go to work. But something has shifted.
For the first time, you could say no. And your boss knows it.

## [02:30] The first year
> SCENE: Your street. Every shop window. Price tags flip up.
Now it spreads. Everyone on your street has more money.
So here is the obvious worry. Will the shops just raise their prices?
Guess. Double? Ten percent more?
> SCENE: A price tag wobbles, and barely moves.
In Kenya, a study poured cash into a whole region. About USD 1,000, once, to over 10,500 poor households (c11).
Prices rose by 0.1% on average. Even at the peak, less than 1% (c10).
The shops did not get greedy. They got busier.
> SCENE: Iran. Crowds at bank machines.
Then there is Iran. It paid everyone, straight into their bank accounts, and the payment was about 29% of a typical household's income (c12).
Researchers found no sign it made people work less, except young people (c13).
But inflation in Iran soared, from 12.4% to 34.7% (c14).
The researchers say the main cause was energy prices, which jumped at the same time (c14). Plenty of Iranians blamed the cash anyway.
That is the honest answer. Small local payments barely move prices. A whole country changing at once is harder to read.
> SCENE: Madhya Pradesh, India. A village lane. Girls in school uniform walk past.
Closer to home, a pilot in Madhya Pradesh gave each adult 200 rupees a month, and 100 for each child (c15).
In those villages, 66% of secondary-age girls were in school, against 36% in villages without it (c16).
In the tribal village, women's work went up by 16% (c17).
Picture what that means on your street. A girl who might have been sent out to earn stays in class instead.
The money does not only buy things. It buys a little room to choose.

## [03:30] The catch
> SCENE: You on the sofa on a Tuesday afternoon, looking at your phone, a little guilty.
By now you might think the lazy-people worry is a myth. It is not quite.
Be honest with yourself. If the money kept coming, would you work a few hours less? Plenty of people would.
In a large US trial, people on low incomes got $1,000 a month for three years (c18).
They earned about $1,900 a year less than the comparison group, and 4.2 points fewer of them were working (c18).
And the time they gained? Mostly leisure (c19).
So free money does change work. Not as much as people fear. More than its fans admit.
> SCENE: A balance scale: on one side a coin, on the other a tiny hourglass.

## [04:30] The long run
> SCENE: The whole country seen from above. A giant bill flutters down towards the treasury building.
Now the real question. Who pays?
India's Economic Survey of 2016 to 2017 worked out a basic income of 7,620 rupees a year per person (c20).
Paying it to 75% of people would cost about 4.9% of GDP (c21).
> SCENE: The finance minister, sweating, holding a calculator.
That is a modest income, and it is already a huge bill.
The OECD looked at rich countries. A basic income paid for without new taxes would be far too small to end poverty. One big enough would be very expensive (c22).
So every country that tries this hits the same choice. Small and affordable, or big and costly.
> SCENE: A fork in the road; two signs.
And even the good news gets argued over.
In a Canadian town in the 1970s, hospital visits fell 8.5% (c25, c23).
A 2021 reanalysis of the same data found the opposite (c24).
That is what following a what-if honestly looks like. Some answers are solid. Some are still being fought over.
Here is what the trials mostly agree on. People do not stop working in droves. They change what they work at.
And they say no to things more often.

## [06:15] Back to you
> SCENE: A year later. You at your own tea stall, or your old desk, your choice. Your phone buzzes again.
So, one year in. The money lands again.
You still go to work. But you picked which work.
The tea stall down the road is still open. The girl in the lane is still in school.
And the argument about who pays is still going on.
The real change was never the money. It was what you could afford to refuse.
