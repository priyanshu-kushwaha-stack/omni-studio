## Premise

One morning a small glowing counter floats over every head you can see: how many lies that person has told since midnight. It clicks and ticks up by one the instant a lie leaves their mouth, and the brighter the number, the more it glows. It resets at midnight. Only you can see it, and only in the room with you, never on a screen. A lie is a thing said to be believed that the speaker knows is false: jokes, stories and being wrong don't count. And it counts what is spoken, not what is typed or written. Your own counter you cannot see. You check the mirror, the phone camera, a shop window: nothing. It isn't on your head. It's in your eyes.

**The catch.** You expect a city lit up like a scoreboard, because you have heard you are lied to a hundred times a day. You get a city of dim zeros and ones, and a handful of heads blazing in the dozens: the five percent, and one of them is your boss. For a day the power is pure sport: you call every bluff, pick the honest mechanic, read a first date's height off his head, win the poker night, and ten out of ten on live television. Then the city learns what you can do, and the city adapts. Everyone around you goes quiet and starts texting, because typed lies don't count. The powerful stop saying anything out loud: the leader everyone is about to vote for holds up a written statement and answers every question with "as I wrote". A rival appears on television claiming your gift with a thermal camera, and the public can't tell you two apart, because the average untrained person tells a lie from the truth barely better than a coin flip, about fifty-four percent of the time (c06). And the question the whole country starts asking you, you can't answer: how many have you told today? The one number you can't see is the one that counts against you.

**Why this setting.** A city nobody names, in the rooms everyone on earth has sat in: a kitchen table, a bus, an open-plan office, a garage, a restaurant, a party, a football stadium, a television studio. The premise needs crowds and faces, not a place, and every fact in the ledger is about people in general: a US national survey of a thousand adults (c03, c04), a diary study of students and neighbours (c01), eighty online daters measured in a lab (c07), a meta-analysis of twenty-four thousand judges (c06), a government report on the polygraph (c09). The alternative weighed was a school, the channel's best-performing room so far; it lost because the analysis asks this film to go to scale (a city, live television, a leader at a podium), and a school's biggest liar is a teenager, while an office's is a man with a corner office and a bonus speech, which is the funnier number to open on. Nothing in the telling needs a local word: a flat, a bus, a boss, a mechanic, a date, a match, a debate.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: Your flat, your bus, your office floor and your city, where you say "fine", "nearly there" and "love it" a dozen times a day and have never once called any of them a lie.
> DESIRE: To never be fooled again, by anyone.
> CONFLICT: You can see the number over every head, and once the city knows it, everyone around you stops talking, the powerful stop speaking out loud, and the one number you cannot see is the one the whole country starts asking you for.
> CHANGE: You stop reading the numbers for your own sport and make the biggest liar in the country say his lies out loud, on air, line by line.
> RESULT: The country watches a number climb that only you can see, believes you anyway, and the next morning somebody reads the number over your own head back to you.
> UNDER: If you could see every lie but your own, would you still want to look?
> DARLING: "The city isn't lying to you. The city is mostly fine. The five percent has a corner office."
> DARLING: "You don't need to see your own. You know what you say."
> DARLING: "Read it out loud. For the people who can't see."
