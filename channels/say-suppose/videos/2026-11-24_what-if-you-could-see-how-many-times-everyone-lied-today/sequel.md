# Sequel: What If Everyone Could See How Many Lies You Told?

## The hook (the last scene, no new rule)

The kitchen, the frame of 0:00 turned round, the morning after the debate. Your flatmate has just said "Nine?" and you have not answered. You put the mug down, pick up the lanyard, go out past the full sink. On the stairs the kid from downstairs, chocolate to the ears, looks up at the air over your head and holds up nine fingers, which takes both hands. The bus: forty heads, and a dozen of them tilted up, looking at the air over each other, patting their own hair the way your flatmate did on the first morning. At the back, the crimson stranger in the long coat, headphones on, counts the bus on their fingers, looks at you, and says the only line the hook needs: "Nine. You're doing well." You cannot see it. The film explains nothing. The viewer who comes back for Part 2 remembers one thing: it spread, overnight, to everyone, and the first number the city learned to read was yours.

## The rule twist, one line

The same counter, over the same heads, now seen by everyone: every person in the city can read every number but their own, so the city that spent Part 1 being read by you now reads itself, and the only way anyone learns their own number is to ask, out loud, someone who can lie.

## The opening skit

Part 2 opens the way Part 1 does: a short acted skit before the narrator speaks. The Monday meeting room, blinds down, the frame of the thumbnail. The boss at the head of the table, a 0 over him, the BOSS mug, and in his hands a laminated card held up for the table: NOBODY'S LOSING THEIR DESK. Fourteen heads nod. He looks round the table at the air over each one, counting on his fingers. He gets to you, large at the left, mug halfway up, and stops. He points. Boss: "Three. Before nine o'clock." Fourteen heads turn to the air over you. You said "fine" to the receptionist, the lift and the kettle. Then he grins, hand on heart: "I fought for every one of these bonuses." Click. The 0 becomes a 1, and the whole table sees it, and the narrator starts.

## Five beats for Part 2

1. **Everyone looks up.** Tuesday, the week after the debate. Your flatmate at breakfast looks at your head and not your face; the bus is forty people reading forty numbers; the stand-up at the whiteboard is silent because everyone is counting everyone. Your habit from Part 1, "fine", "nearly there", "love it", is now the number the whole floor reads off you by ten o'clock. Title question by 0:15: what if everyone could see how many lies you told? The rule in three lines, with its flagged limit: every number is public but your own, it still counts only what is spoken, and every counter resets at midnight, so a day's number dies with the day. Remember that.

2. **Ask someone who can lie.** The montage turns at every use. You can't see your own number, so you ask, and you watch the asker's head: "Am I on zero?" "Yes." Click. A café sells "ask me" badges; the receptionist answers everyone's number for a pound; the boss finds the one honest person on the floor and keeps her at his desk as a mirror; first dates open with "What am I on?" and end at the next table's answer. The gap every time: you expect the city to go honest and it goes quiet, then goes to the keyboard, then learns the mirror trick and uses it on you. A loophole for the comments: if you ask a liar your number and he lies, does his click tell you the truth?

3. **The kind ones stop.** At the midpoint the wrong belief breaks: you thought the number was a measure of a person, and it was a measure of their company. The facts are mirrors on an ordinary thing, the white lie: how most lies told to close friends are kind ones, what happened in a study where people were told to stop lying for weeks, what a conversation sounds like when nobody softens anything. Your flatmate stops saying he did the dishes and stops saying good morning too. The kid downstairs stops talking. The challenger, in office now, is on four a day and the only politician anyone trusts, because four is a number a person can believe.

4. **The honest one goes quiet.** The cost at 60%. Your flatmate would rather say nothing than let his number climb where you can see it, so the two of you eat cereal in silence for a week, and the one kind lie he ever told you, "you'd had a bad week", is the last thing he said that wasn't a fact. On Friday he asks the question only you can answer: "It wasn't me, was it? The bad week." Dilemma at 75%: tell him the kind lie, with the whole floor's worth of people on the bus able to watch your head click where you can't see it, or tell him the truth, and be honest to the honest one, and lose him for it.

5. **Midnight.** The flagged limit pays off: the whole city in the square at 11:59, every head reading every other head, the counters bright as frost. You answer him at 11:59:50, out loud, the kind lie, and he watches your counter click to ten while you watch his face. The clock strikes. Every number in the square goes to zero at once, including yours, and he is the only one who saw it, and he says the line from the bus turned round: "Zero. You're doing well." Your gag from Part 1 returns in his mouth: "Say it again?" You laugh, and you don't say a word. The residue, inside the last image of the square at one minute past midnight, sixty thousand zeros: tomorrow, when someone tells you you're on zero, you'll wonder for the first time whether they are reading the number or being kind.
