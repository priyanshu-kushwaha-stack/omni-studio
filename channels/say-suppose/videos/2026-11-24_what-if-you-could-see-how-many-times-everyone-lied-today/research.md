# Research: What If You Saw How Many Lies Everyone Told? (G2)

paid APIs) and returned verbatim quotes; the session re-read c03 and c12 at the source at G1 and read each quote against

## The wrong belief the film turns on

The popular line, from a 2011 TED talk, is that "you may be lied to anywhere from 10 to 200 times" a day; the talk
names no study (Meyer, https://www.ted.com/talks/pamela_meyer_how_to_spot_a_liar/transcript, c12). The large studies
say otherwise. A national survey asked 1,000 US adults to report their lies in the past 24 hours: "Sixty percent of
subjects report telling no lies at all, and almost half of all lies are told by only 5% of subjects" (Serota, Levine
& Boster 2010, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2009.01366.x, c03). The same authors' 2021 paper repeats the figures
exactly, 59.9% and 5.3%, with an average of 1.65 lies a day pulled up by the few (https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2021-serota.pdf,
c04). A 91-day diary study of 632 people found "most lies were told by a few prolific liars" and "almost 90% of all
lies were little white lies" (Serota, Levine & Docan-Morgan, https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2021.1985153, c15, c17).
So the film's world is mostly zeros and ones, and the turn at the stadium (frost, not fire; a few people doing the
lying for everyone) is the research, not a twist for its own sake.

## The everyday rates

The older diary studies give the familiar "a lie or two a day": "77 college students reported telling 2 lies a day,
and 70 community members told 1" (DePaulo et al. 1996, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8656340/, c01). The film says
"reported", since these are self-reports. People tell fewer lies per conversation to people they feel close to, and
more of those are kind (DePaulo & Kashy 1998, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.1.63, c14): that is the flatmate's
"you'd had a bad week". The strangers figure (about 60% lied at least once in a ten-minute chat) comes from a press
release about one experiment with students, some told to seem likable or competent
(https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2002/06/020611070813.htm, c05), so it is `contested` and the line says "in one
study of students" and that some had been told to make a good impression.

## The film's limit: spoken, not typed

A seven-day diary study found people "lied most on the telephone and least in email", with instant messages about as
often as face to face (Hancock, Thom-Santelli & Ritchie 2004, https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985709, c11). The film uses
both halves: the phone call on the bus, and on the silent Tuesday the honest reading that people did not stop lying
when they went to their keyboards; the counter just can't hear them. It never says people lie less in writing.

## The set pieces

- Dating: of 80 online daters, 81% were off on at least one of height, weight or age, usually by little (an average
  of 0.77 inch among those who shaded their height) (Toma, Hancock & Ellison 2008,
  https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/events/shb/2009/hancock2.pdf, c07). Hence "everybody's lying by two inches" as a joke the
  numbers sit under.
- Children: in a resistance-to-temptation test, most children aged 3 to 8 peeked at a toy and later lied about it
  (Talwar & Lee 2008, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18717895/, c16); in a study of 2- and 3-year-olds, the older
  peekers more often denied it, and "children begin to tell lies at a very young age" (Evans & Lee 2013,
  https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031409, c08). The film says "developmental studies" and "around age 2 to 3".
- Detection: untrained people are right about 54% of the time telling lies from truths, and lean towards believing
  (Bond & DePaulo 2006 meta-analysis, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/, c06). The 4:00 card carries the one
  number, 54% (the room's G1 note).
- The rival's nose: a 2012 press release claimed the nose warms when you lie
  (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/12/121203081834.htm, c13); a 2017 thermography paper found nose
  temperature tended to fall (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28714186/, c18). Both `contested`; the film states no
  direction as fact, only that they disagree ("Nobody's nose is a counter").
- The polygraph: "little basis for the expectation that a polygraph test could have extremely high accuracy" (National
  Research Council 2003, https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10420/chapter/2, c09), with the same report's caveat
  that specific-incident tests beat chance, which the line keeps.

## Why a city with no name

The brief's target 5 (told at scale, in a world anyone knows): a flat, a bus, an office, a garage, a first date, a
stadium and a televised debate exist everywhere, so the film names no country and needs none. The surveys are American
and the film says so where it cites them ("a US national survey").
