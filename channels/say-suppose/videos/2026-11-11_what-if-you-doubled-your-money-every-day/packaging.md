## Premise

Whatever is in your bank account at midnight is doubled by morning, starting from the one-rupee coin your grandmother sends you on your birthday, and it will not stop.
The catch has two halves: the new money is brand-new, taken from nobody, and the only off switch is a balance of exactly zero at midnight.

Invented: the doubling, the split-flap counter on the chai stall wall, your family. Real: every number on the counter (two to the power of thirty on day thirty-one), the chessboard legend, how badly people guess doubling, that new money raises prices first where it is spent, that repaying a bank loan makes money stop existing, and how much cash and how many loans India actually has.

## The viewer

Riya, 22, Pune, watching on a phone on the metro home and then in bed. "You" are a final-year student with a part-time internship who is broke by the twentieth of every month and runs a chai tab in Mangesh bhaiya's notebook outside college. The cast: **Kabir** (17, your younger cousin, lives with you, films everything, says "bro" to his grandmother), **Dadi** (your grandmother, keeps forty years of savings in a steel dabba, gives every gift ending in one), **Baba** (your father, just retired from the electricity board, a pension and eleven EMIs left on the flat, counts them), **Mangesh bhaiya** (the chaiwala, notebook of tabs, cart on EMI), **Mr. Deshmukh** (the branch manager, two phone calls, calls you "sir" once the number has eight digits). Aai calls twice a day, voice only. The crimson stranger stands in the chai queue once, holding a one-rupee coin, for the second watch.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: You are twenty-two in Pune, broke by the twentieth of every month, running a chai tab in Mangesh bhaiya's notebook.
> DESIRE: You want, just once, to pay for the whole lane and never say "likh lo" again.
> CONFLICT: Your balance doubles every night and will not stop, and every rupee you push out raises the prices on your own lane, starting with what Dadi's dabba can buy.
> CHANGE: You learn the one place money disappears instead of landing in someone's pocket, and that stopping means sending away the rupee that started it.
> RESULT: Zero at midnight; you are back at the stall asking for a tab, and Dadi's phone reads two.
> UNDER: If I had all the money, would there be anything left to buy?

**The rule device.** A split-flap counter on the chai stall wall, DAY on the left, BALANCE on the right. It clacks at every midnight, the flaps add digits as the number grows, and from day forty-seven the flaps run off the board the way the digits run off your phone in the cold open. It is the thing we cut to instead of a graph, so every stake jump is a sound before it is a number.

**The running gag and its returns.** Dadi's one rupee. (1) Birthday, 0:55: one rupee "for the part that never ends", and it is the seed. (2) Day twenty-seven, 3:15: you give her one crore and one; "You remembered the one." (3) 6:05: she presses ₹101 from the halved dabba into your hand, "the one keeps it going", and the gag turns into the cost. (4) 8:30: the last rupee on the counter is hers, and you send it back marked "for the part that ends"; the peak is you choosing the ending her saying never has. (5) 9:10: her phone reads one, then two.

**The flagged limit and how it pays off.** At 0:50: "There is no off switch, except a balance of exactly zero at midnight. Remember that." It pays twice. The boring sentence at 3:05 (repaying a loan makes money stop existing) is the only route to zero that doesn't land in someone's pocket, and the day-forty-eight numbers from the research make that route expire at midnight: the last night the country's loans are bigger than your balance. "Exactly zero" is also why keeping one rupee is a real choice you refuse.

**The open loops, about a minute apart.** 0:28, "nothing you did could stop it" (so how does it stop?). 0:50, "Remember that." 1:25, Dadi's "part that never ends" (will it?). 2:30, Kabir's month-end guess of twenty lakh ("keep that number"). 3:10, "the most important boring sentence in this video" (why?). 3:45, Mangesh bhaiya rewrites the board ("watch the board"). 5:05, "after tonight you never get richer again; nobody tells you." 6:15, "every gift has an address, and the address pays." 7:00, "tomorrow it's bigger than every loan in India" (then what?). 7:30, "exactly zero. Not one."

**Chapters (8).** 1. Day Thirty-One. 2. The One That Keeps It Going. 3. Nothing, Nothing, Nothing, Phone. 4. Sir. 5. The Second Half of the Board. 6. Your Double, Her Half. 7. Giving Makes It Worse. 8. Zero at Midnight.

**The sequel.** Part 2: the rupee you returned doubles in Dadi's untouched account, and a woman who keeps her life savings in a steel box now has the glitch and a notebook full of people she has promised gifts ending in one. She knows nothing about day forty-eight; you do, and you have forty-seven days to get her to zero without telling her why.

**The viewer's five "but what about" questions.**
1. Why not just keep it small, give the extra away each night from day fifteen? Answered at 2:35: you could, and you don't, because for the first time in your life the number is going up, and Wagenaar and Sagaria is why you can't feel what it becomes.
2. Why not withdraw it all in cash and burn it? Answered at 6:25: by day forty-seven there is less cash in India than your balance, and the bank has been reporting you since day twenty-five.
3. Why not spend it abroad, or on gold? Answered at 5:35: wherever you spend, that market's price breaks first; Mansa Musa did it with gold in Cairo.
4. Why not hand it to the government? Answered at 7:15 in one line: governments spend; that is what they are for; it lands in pockets, and the pockets chase the same samosas.
5. Doesn't repaying a loan just give the money to the bank? Answered at 3:05 and again at 7:20 by the boring sentence: a repaid loan and the money that repays it cancel; it stops existing.
Left open for the comments: does the doubling follow the account or you? Nobody in the film opens a second account, moves it to cash under the mattress, or goes overdrawn to see whether a negative number doubles.

**Past 8 minutes, why a viewer stays.** At 7:35 there is a clock on screen and a number that has to hit exactly zero, read down by a seventeen-year-old. At 8:20 it reads 946, then 1, and the viewer knows whose rupee that is before you do. The wordless peak holds Dadi's phone and the coin, and the viewer has been waiting to see her face since 6:05.

**How this differs from "What IF You Had Infinite Money?"** Theirs is a bottomless card and a spending ladder; ours is one coin you can count night by night on a split-flap counter, with a day the arithmetic itself turns into a deadline. Their cost is a mother counting coins at a corner store and a dollar falling on television; ours is prices on your own lane moving first, a chaiwala closing his notebook, and a grandmother's steel box, in rupees, in Pune. Their climax is scissors on a card at a countdown with cameras; ours is quietly repaying ordinary people's loans because that is the one place money stops existing, down to a single rupee you send back to the person who gave it.
