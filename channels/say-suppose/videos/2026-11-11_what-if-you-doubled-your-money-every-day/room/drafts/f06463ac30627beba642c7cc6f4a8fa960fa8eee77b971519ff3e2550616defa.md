# Packaging: doubled your money every day

topic: second /say-suppose-topics slate (2026-11-11), f-money-doubles, ranked 1st at 90/100, proposed by Claude Fable

## Premise

Whatever is in your bank account at midnight is doubled by morning, starting from the one-dollar coin your grandmother sends you on your birthday, and it will not stop: in a month it buys the pier you live on, by day forty-eight it is bigger than everything the whole world makes in a year (about $128 trillion, the IMF's 2026 projection; claims.md c01), and by day fifty-eight it overflows the number box most computers count in (c06).
The catch has two halves: the new money is brand-new, taken from nobody, so every dollar of it quietly thins everyone else's; and the only off switch is a balance of exactly zero at midnight.

Why this setting: a port city on a bay, in dollars. The world's yearly output and the world's debt are counted in dollars, so the night the planet can no longer swallow you is a real figure on a real calendar; and a harbour is where the stuff money is a claim on actually arrives, so the counter has a pier to run down and ships to be measured against. No country is named; the story reads the same anywhere.

Invented: the doubling, the split-flap counter that grows a flap for every digit, your family, the bank's number box (said as story). Real, each with a row in `claims.md`: the counting times (c08), the chessboard's numbers (c07), how badly people guess doubling (c05), that new money raises prices first where it is spent (c10), that repaying a bank loan makes money stop existing (c03, c04), what the whole world makes in a year (c01) and how much it owes (c02), Mansa Musa's gold (c11), the nineteen-digit box most computers count in (c06), and the day the dollars would outnumber the atoms in the observable universe (c09; in the video description only, never spoken). The balance on the counter is the doubling's own arithmetic (two to the power of thirty on day thirty-one).

## Cast

**You**: anyone with a phone, a pier and a tab at the kiosk, who looks at the balance before buying anything. **Theo**: your younger cousin, seventeen, lives in the flat, films everything, writes ten-line programs. **Nana**: your grandmother, forty years of savings in a biscuit tin, every gift she gives carries one extra dollar and the same four words. **Mrs. Vance**: runs the kiosk at the foot of the pier, coffee and papers, a notebook of tabs, eleven payments left on the kiosk; the counter hangs on her wall. **Mr. Halloran**: the branch manager, two phone calls, calls you "sir" once the number has ten digits.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: You live above a pier kiosk in a port city with your grandmother and your cousin, and you look at your balance before you buy anything, even a coffee.
> DESIRE: You want the number to go up, just once, far enough that you never have to look at it again.
> CONFLICT: Your balance doubles every midnight and nothing stops it but exactly zero; in forty-nine nights it is bigger than every debt on Earth (c02), and every dollar of it is quietly taken out of everyone else's, starting with your grandmother's biscuit tin.
> CHANGE: You learn the one place money goes to stop existing, and that stopping means breaking the one rule your grandmother ever gave you, on the last night the planet is big enough to swallow the rest.
> RESULT: Zero at midnight; you are back at the kiosk looking at a zero and asking for a tab.
> UNDER: If I had all the money, would there be anything left to buy?

**The five "but what about" questions.**
1. Why not just keep it small, send the extra away every night? Answered at 2:45: you could, and you open the transfer screen, and you don't, because for the first time in your life the number is going up, and Wagenaar and Sagaria is why you can't feel what it becomes.
2. Why not withdraw it all in cash and burn it? Answered at 6:10: by day forty-eight your balance is more than everything the world makes in a year (c01); to burn it you'd first have to print it, and the bank has been flagging you every morning since before Mr. Halloran's first call (story).
3. Why not spend it abroad, or on gold? Answered at 5:25: wherever you spend, that market's price breaks first; Mansa Musa did it with gold in Cairo.
4. Why not hand it to a government? Answered at 7:15 in one line: governments spend; that is what they are for; it lands in pockets, and the pockets chase the same coffees.
5. Doesn't repaying a loan just give the money to the bank? Answered at 3:05 and again at 6:50 by the boring sentence: a repaid loan and the money that repays it cancel; it stops existing.
Left open for the comments: whether letting it run to day fifty-eight would have flipped the bank's own box to a minus and cancelled you. Theo's laptop only showed what the computer does, not what the dollar does, and nobody in the film waits to find out.

**Past 8 minutes, why someone watching stays.** At 7:35 there is a clock on screen, a pier full of flaps running backwards over the water, and a number that has to hit exactly zero, read down by a seventeen-year-old on a bike. At 8:20 it reads eleven payments, then one, and whoever is watching knows whose dollar that is before you say it. The wordless peak holds Nana's phone and the coin, and they have been waiting to see her face since 5:50.

**How this differs from "What IF You Had Infinite Money?"** Theirs is a bottomless card and a spending ladder; ours is one coin you can count night by night on a counter that outgrows a wall, a pier and a computer, with a day the arithmetic itself turns into a deadline. Their cost is a mother counting coins at a corner store and a dollar falling on television; ours is prices on your own pier moving first, a kiosk closing its notebook, and a grandmother's biscuit tin. Their climax is scissors on a card at a countdown with cameras; ours is quietly repaying ordinary people's loans because that is the one place money stops existing, down to a single dollar you send back to the person who gave it.
