## Premise

Whatever is in your bank account at midnight is doubled by morning, starting from the one-rupee coin your grandmother sends you on your birthday, and it will not stop: in a month it buys your lane, in seven weeks it is bigger than all the cash in India, and ten nights after that it overflows the number box most computers count in.
The catch has two halves: the new money is brand-new, taken from nobody, so every rupee of it quietly thins everyone else's; and the only off switch is a balance of exactly zero at midnight.

Invented: the doubling, the split-flap counter that grows a flap for every digit, your family, the bank's number box (said as story). Real: every number on the counter (two to the power of thirty on day thirty-one), the chessboard legend, how badly people guess doubling, that new money raises prices first where it is spent, that repaying a bank loan makes money stop existing, how much cash and how many loans India actually has, the nineteen-digit box most computers count in, and the day the rupees would outnumber the atoms in the universe.

## Cast

**You**: anyone with a phone, a lane and a chai tab, who looks at the balance before buying anything. **Kabir**: your younger cousin, lives in the flat, films everything, writes ten-line programs, says "bro" to his grandmother. **Dadi**: your grandmother, forty years of savings in a steel dabba, every gift she gives ends in one. **Baba**: your father, counts things, eleven EMIs left on the flat and he knows the number. **Mangesh bhaiya**: the chaiwala, a notebook of tabs, the counter hangs on his wall. **Mr. Deshmukh**: the branch manager, two phone calls, calls you "sir" once the number has eight digits. **Aai**: voice only, calls twice a day until she calls three times. The crimson stranger stands third in the chai queue once, turning a one-rupee coin, for the second watch.

## Five lines (rules/story.md, check 1)

> SITUATION: You live on a lane in India with your grandmother and your cousin, and you look at your balance before you buy anything, even a glass of chai.
> DESIRE: You want the number to go up, just once, far enough that you never have to look at it again.
> CONFLICT: Your balance doubles every midnight and nothing stops it but exactly zero; in forty-eight nights it is bigger than every loan in the country, and every rupee of it is quietly taken out of everyone else's, starting with your grandmother's steel box.
> CHANGE: You learn the one place money goes to stop existing, and that stopping means sending back the rupee that started it, on the last night the country is big enough to swallow the rest.
> RESULT: Zero at midnight; you are back at the stall looking at a zero and asking for a tab, and your grandmother's phone reads two.
> UNDER: If I had all the money, would there be anything left to buy?

**The rule device.** A split-flap counter on the chai stall wall, DAY on the left, BALANCE on the right. On the repayment night the flaps run back in, the lane reclaiming itself, until one flap is left on the wall. It is the thing we cut to instead of a graph, so every stake jump is a sound and a length before it is a number.

**The three jaw drops.** (1) 4:00, the chessboard: a chai glass of rice at square thirteen, the city buried by the second half, 18 quintillion grains on the last square. **The sequel.** Part 2: the rupee you returned doubles in Dadi's untouched account, and a woman who keeps her life savings in a steel box now has the glitch, a notebook full of people she has promised gifts ending in one, and no loans left in the country to swallow it. She knows nothing about day forty-eight; you do, and you have forty-seven days to get her to zero without telling her why.

**The five "but what about" questions.**
1. Why not just keep it small, give the extra away each night from day fifteen? Answered at 2:40: you could, and you open the transfer screen, and you don't, because for the first time in your life the number is going up, and Wagenaar and Sagaria is why you can't feel what it becomes.
2. Why not withdraw it all in cash and burn it? Answered at 6:25: by day forty-seven there is less cash in India than your balance, and the bank has been reporting you since day twenty-five.
3. Why not spend it abroad, or on gold? Answered at 5:35: wherever you spend, that market's price breaks first; Mansa Musa did it with gold in Cairo.
4. Why not hand it to the government? Answered at 7:15 in one line: governments spend; that is what they are for; it lands in pockets, and the pockets chase the same samosas.
5. Doesn't repaying a loan just give the money to the bank? Answered at 3:05 and again at 7:20 by the boring sentence: a repaid loan and the money that repays it cancel; it stops existing.
Left open for the comments: what a negative balance does at midnight. Nobody in the film goes overdrawn to find out, and Kabir's laptop only showed what the computer does, not what the rupee does.

**Past 8 minutes, why someone watching stays.** At 7:35 there is a clock on screen, a lane full of flaps running backwards, and a number that has to hit exactly zero, read down by a seventeen-year-old on a cycle. At 8:20 it reads eleven plus one, then one, and whoever is watching knows whose rupee that is before you do. The wordless peak holds Dadi's phone and the coin, and they have been waiting to see her face since 6:05.

**How this differs from "What IF You Had Infinite Money?"** Theirs is a bottomless card and a spending ladder; ours is one coin you can count night by night on a counter that outgrows a wall, a lane and a computer, with a day the arithmetic itself turns into a deadline. Their cost is a mother counting coins at a corner store and a dollar falling on television; ours is prices on your own lane moving first, a chaiwala closing his notebook, and a grandmother's steel box. Their climax is scissors on a card at a countdown with cameras; ours is quietly repaying ordinary people's loans because that is the one place money stops existing, down to a single rupee you send back to the person who gave it.
